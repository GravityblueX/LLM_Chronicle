# March 2022

## Chinchilla: Revising the Compute-Optimal Scaling Law

> In the spring of 2022, DeepMind added a hard rule to the large model competition: given the same training compute, more parameters are not always better. Models and data must grow together—a large model trained on too little data is simply wasting compute.

### Release

**2022-03-29** — The DeepMind team published the **Chinchilla** paper, *"Training Compute-Optimal Large Language Models,"* on arXiv. The paper argued that under a fixed compute budget, model parameter count and training token count should grow roughly in proportion; many large language models at the time were over-parameterized and under-trained. Chinchilla used 70B parameters trained on approximately 1.4T tokens. Under the same training compute budget as Gopher (280B parameters, 300B tokens), Chinchilla used roughly 4.7 times more data and comprehensively outperformed Gopher across downstream tasks, achieving 67.5% average accuracy on MMLU (5-shot). arXiv, 2022-03-29[^1]; DeepMind Blog, 2022-04-12[^2]

### Significance

The key takeaway from Chinchilla was not "we built a bigger model," but rather the reverse: **bigger is not always better**. After GPT-3 and Gopher, the industry could easily interpret scaling laws as "keep piling on parameters." Chinchilla exposed the crack in that reasoning—if training tokens are insufficient, parameter count becomes an expensive hollow shell.

This lesson left a deep mark. Llama, Llama 2, Claude, Gemini, and subsequent open-source models all placed greater emphasis on the balance between "parameters, data, and compute" rather than reporting a single parameter number. It also brought inference cost back into the heart of model design: a 70B model trained more thoroughly could be more useful, cheaper, and easier to deploy than a 280B model.

### Commentary

Chinchilla was a rare "cooling" event in the history of large language models. It did not push the competition toward ever more extravagant parameter counts; instead, it told everyone: settle the math first. Anyone who has spent a decade building a city understands this—more bricks are not always better. Foundation, materials, and construction schedule must be balanced; if any one of them falls out of alignment, cracks will eventually appear at the base of the wall.
