# January 2022

## InstructGPT: Teaching Models to Follow Instructions

> GPT-3 proved that large language models could continue text, but it did not prove they could "do what people mean." InstructGPT introduced human preferences into the training pipeline, shifting language models from text completers to instruction-following assistants. The explosion of ChatGPT later that year was first paved here.

### Release

**2022-01-27** — OpenAI published the research blog post **"Aligning language models to follow instructions"**, introducing InstructGPT: a family of instruction-following models created by fine-tuning GPT-3 with human feedback.[^1]

On March 4 of the same year, the OpenAI team submitted the paper **"Training language models to follow instructions with human feedback"** to arXiv. The paper described the training pipeline in three steps: first, collect human annotator demonstrations and perform supervised fine-tuning (SFT); then, have annotators compare multiple model outputs and train a reward model; finally, use reinforcement learning from human feedback to further optimize the model. The paper explicitly referred to these models as **InstructGPT**.[^2]

### Why InstructGPT Was Needed

GPT-3's problem was not that it "couldn't talk," but that it "didn't know how the user wanted it to talk." When a user asked a question, GPT-3 might simply continue the prompt; when a user requested factual information, it might fabricate; when a user expected safety boundaries, it might not know to refuse. OpenAI stated the problem directly in the paper's abstract: larger language models do not naturally follow user intent better—they may produce outputs that are untruthful, toxic, or unhelpful.[^2]

The key innovation of InstructGPT was not more parameters, but a changed objective. It no longer simply learned "what token is most likely next," but instead learned "which response humans prefer more." The paper reported that on OpenAI's prompt distribution, **the output of the 1.3B-parameter InstructGPT was preferred by human evaluators over the 175B-parameter GPT-3**, despite having roughly 100 times fewer parameters. Meanwhile, InstructGPT also showed improvements in truthfulness and reduced harmful outputs, though it still made simple errors.[^2]

### Alignment Transforms from Research to Production Readiness

InstructGPT brought an idea previously used mainly in reinforcement learning environments into the industrial pipeline of language models. In 2017, research by OpenAI and DeepMind had already demonstrated that reinforcement learning agents could be trained using human preferences over trajectory segments; this work even showed that in Atari games and simulated robotics tasks, complex behaviors could be trained with less than 1% of the agent's interaction volume in human feedback.[^3]

With InstructGPT, this approach was transplanted to text generation: humans did not directly write out all reference answers, but instead provided demonstrations, compared preferences, and let a reward model summarize those preferences. Thus, "alignment" was no longer just a buzzword in research papers but had become a reusable production pipeline: pre-trained model → supervised fine-tuning → preference modeling → RLHF.

This pipeline led directly to ChatGPT. ChatGPT used GPT-3.5 that had been instruction-tuned and RLHF-trained; it made ordinary users feel, for the first time, that the model was "answering me" rather than "continuing my text." If ChatGPT was the consumer product explosion of November 2022, InstructGPT was the prehistory of its training regime.

### Commentary

InstructGPT's historical significance is easily overshadowed by the brilliance of ChatGPT. But without InstructGPT, ChatGPT would have been merely a smoother text-completion interface, not a trustworthy assistant for the general public.

What InstructGPT truly changed was not the upper bound of model capabilities, but the contract between model and user. Pre-trained models asked "what does language typically come next?"; InstructGPT began asking "how does the user want me to respond?" This shift in the question mattered more than the parameter gap between 1.3 billion and 175 billion. It made "becoming more obedient" an engineering goal that could, for the first time, be trained, evaluated, and deployed. The barrier for large language models to move from research artifacts to consumer products was not larger parameters, but a clearer understanding of human preferences.
