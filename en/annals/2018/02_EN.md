# February 2018

## ELMo: The Last Glory of Word Embeddings

> Before contextualized representations became standard, ELMo showed that words could have different meanings in different contexts — and that this mattered.

### Paper Publication

**2018-02-15** — "Deep contextualized word representations" (ELMo) was published on arXiv by Matthew E. Peters, Mark Neumann, Mohit Iyyer, Matt Gardner, Christopher Clark, Kenton Lee, and Luke Zettlemoyer from the Allen Institute for Artificial Intelligence.[^1]

ELMo (Embeddings from Language Models) was a breakthrough in word representation. Previous word embeddings like Word2Vec and GloVe assigned a single fixed vector to each word, regardless of context. "Bank" would have the same representation whether it meant a financial institution or a river bank.

ELMo solved this by using a bidirectional LSTM language model to generate contextualized embeddings. Each word's representation depended on the entire sentence, capturing polysemy and context-dependent meaning.

### Technical Approach

ELMo's architecture was straightforward:

- **Character-level CNN**: Generated initial word representations from characters, handling out-of-vocabulary words
- **Bidirectional LSTM**: Two layers of forward and backward LSTMs processed the sequence
- **Layer-wise representations**: The final embedding was a weighted sum of all LSTM layers, where weights were learned for specific tasks

The key insight was that different layers captured different linguistic information. Lower layers captured syntax, while higher layers captured semantics. Task-specific weighting allowed models to use the most relevant representations.

### Impact and Legacy

ELMo achieved state-of-the-art results on six NLP benchmarks:

- Question answering (SQuAD)
- Sentiment analysis (SST)
- Named entity recognition (CoNLL)
- Semantic role labeling (OntoNotes)
- Coreference resolution (CoNLL)
- Syntactic parsing (Penn Treebank)

More importantly, ELMo demonstrated that **contextualized representations** were superior to static embeddings. This insight directly influenced BERT, GPT, and all subsequent pre-trained language models.

However, ELMo's reliance on LSTMs limited its scalability. Within months, Transformer-based models would supersede it, making ELMo a historical milestone rather than a lasting architecture.

---

### Commentary

ELMo was the last word embedding model before the Transformer revolution. It showed that context mattered, that words weren't fixed points in vector space but shifting meanings dependent on their surroundings. This was a crucial conceptual step, even if the technical implementation was quickly overtaken.

The transition from ELMo to BERT wasn't just an architectural change — it was a paradigm shift from "feature extraction" to "pre-training + fine-tuning." ELMo provided embeddings as features for downstream models; BERT and its successors became the models themselves.
