# November 2023

## DeepSeek-LLM 67B: DeepSeek's open-source entrance

> An AI team incubated by a Chinese quantitative fund knocked on the door of open-source LLMs for the first time with a 67B-parameter model — surpassing Llama 2, matching GPT-3.5 — but what truly mattered was the path it would take afterward.

### Release

**2023-11-29** — DeepSeek (founded by Liang Wenfeng, the founder of High-Flyer Quant) officially released **DeepSeek-LLM**, comprising 7B and 67B sizes, with both Base and Chat versions open-sourced. This was the DeepSeek team's first publicly available large language model. Code was released under the MIT license; model weights were released under the DeepSeek Model Agreement. GitHub: deepseek-ai/DeepSeek-LLM[^1]; arXiv:2401.02954, submitted 2024-01-05[^2]

The models were trained from scratch on a 2-trillion-token Chinese-English bilingual dataset. The Base model surpassed Llama 2 70B across reasoning, coding, math, and Chinese comprehension. The Chat model was aligned using SFT + DPO (Direct Preference Optimization, rather than the then-mainstream RLHF), and surpassed GPT-3.5 in open-ended evaluations.[^2]

### Technical significance

DeepSeek-LLM's technical contributions ran along two lines:

**Scaling law research**: The paper systematically studied scaling laws under open-source configurations (7B and 67B), providing independent verification of compute-data ratios for mid-scale models after Chinchilla. The DeepSeek team proposed the concept of "Longtermism" — training data and architecture design should not be optimized for a single release, but for the scalability of future iterations.

**DPO as an alternative to RLHF**: The Chat model used DPO for alignment, without relying on RLHF's reward model training and PPO optimization. Rafailov et al.'s DPO paper (arXiv:2305.18290) had been published only half a year earlier; DeepSeek was among the first teams to apply it at the 67B scale. This "lightweight alignment" paradigm was later pushed to its extreme in DeepSeek-V2 and R1 — R1-Zero even skipped SFT entirely.

### Assessment

DeepSeek-LLM itself was not the brightest star. 67B parameters, surpassing Llama 2, matching GPT-3.5 — these were "respectable" rather than "stunning" in the open-source LLM race of late 2023. Had DeepSeek stopped here, it would have been just another Chinese company that "also made a 70B open-source model."

But it did not stop.

From LLM to V2 (2024-05), then to V3 (2024-12) and R1 (2025-01), DeepSeek followed a clear trajectory: **every generation treated efficiency as the core metric, not parameter count.** The scaling law research in the LLM paper was the starting point of this path — "first figure out how to train efficiently, then talk about scaling up." This methodology was consistently applied in the subsequent V2 (MoE + MLA to reduce inference costs), V3 ($5.6M training cost), and R1 (pure RL to elicit reasoning).

Viewed from the perspective of building a city over a decade, DeepSeek-LLM was the foundation. Not glamorous, but solid. Without it, there would have been no V2, V3, or R1. Every team's every step begins with the first shovel of earth.

### Commentary

DeepSeek-LLM's release did not cause much of a stir in the Chinese AI community at the end of 2023. All eyes were on ChatGPT's first anniversary (2023-11-30) and the Llama 2 open-source ecosystem — who could have imagined that one year later, the name "DeepSeek" would become the most discussed topic across all of Silicon Valley?

This paper lacked the "explosive" data of the later V3 and R1 — $5.6M training cost, MIT open-source, pure RL emergent reasoning. But read carefully, it already displayed the three most distinctive characteristics of the DeepSeek team. First, **no worship of parameter count** — 67B benchmarked against 70B, not because they couldn't go larger, but because "it was enough." Second, **the lightweight alignment approach** — using DPO instead of RLHF, signaling a wariness toward pipeline complexity. Third, **open source** — model weights were publicly released from day one, which was uncommon among domestic LLM projects at the time.

These characteristics were "choices" at the time. Two years later, in retrospect, they were "inevitabilities."
