# March 2023

## GPT-4: The frontier of multimodality, and a door left unopened

> Outscoring 90% of bar exam takers, earning 700 on SAT Math, capable of interpreting images and writing poetry from memes. GPT-4 defined what "frontier" meant in March 2023 — but this time, OpenAI told no one how large the model actually was.

### Release

**2023-03-14** — OpenAI released **GPT-4**, the first multimodal GPT model — supporting image input (static images) and text output.[^1]

GPT-4's release adopted a "quiet rollout" strategy: no launch event, no livestream — just a blog post and a technical report. But the content itself was staggering — GPT-4 scored higher than 90% of takers on the Uniform Bar Exam, earned 710/800 on SAT Evidence-Based Reading & Writing, and received a perfect score of 5 on AP Biology.

A conspicuous blank stood out in the technical report: **no disclosed parameter count**, no disclosed training data volume, no disclosed model architecture. This was a first for the GPT series — GPT-1, GPT-2, and GPT-3 all disclosed their parameter counts. OpenAI cited "competitive landscape and safety implications" as the reason, but the community widely interpreted it as: the parameter count was too large, and disclosure would trigger safety panic and regulatory scrutiny.

External estimates (based on leaked information and reverse engineering): GPT-4 was approximately 1.76 trillion parameters, using a **Mixture of Experts (MoE) architecture** composed of 8 expert models of roughly 220B each, with 2 activated per inference.[^2] This estimate was never confirmed by OpenAI.

### Multimodal capabilities

GPT-4 could accept images as input and understand, describe, and reason about them. Demonstration examples included:[^1]

- Explaining the humor of a meme composed of multiple images stitched together
- Generating corresponding HTML code for a hand-drawn sketch
- Recommending recipes based on available ingredients from a photo of the inside of a refrigerator
- Analyzing charts, reading handwritten text, and identifying objects in photographs

However, there was a limitation: GPT-4 could only read images, not generate them. Full "read + write" multimodal capability would not arrive until GPT-4o a year later (2024-05).

### Safety and alignment

Nearly half of GPT-4's technical report (approximately 50 pages) was devoted to safety evaluations and mitigation measures — a rarity among large model papers at the time.[^3]

Key measures:
- **Red-teaming**: Six months of external expert attempts to breach safety guardrails prior to release
- **RLHF + Rule-based reward model**: Improved alignment training to reduce harmful outputs
- **System message**: Allowing developers to control model behavior through system prompts

At launch, OpenAI claimed GPT-4 improved safety metrics by 82% over GPT-3.5 (reducing harmful responses to sensitive requests). However, this figure was later disputed — "jailbreak" cases in actual use emerged in endless succession, including the widespread propagation of the DAN (Do Anything Now) mode.

### Deep integration with Microsoft

The relationship between GPT-4 and New Bing forms a complex timeline. When Microsoft launched New Bing on 2023-02-07, it publicly claimed to use "a new generation OpenAI large model" (internal codename Prometheus Model), without specifying whether it was GPT-3.5 or GPT-4. Independent reporting (The Verge) and the leaked Sydney prompt manual both pointed to the initial model being based on GPT-3.5. Microsoft CTO Kevin Scott later stated that New Bing "was GPT-4 from the start," but this conflicted with the community's technical analysis at the time (the prompt manual explicitly stated "based on GPT-3.5").

The more likely scenario: New Bing launched with an enhanced version of GPT-3.5 and switched to GPT-4 shortly after GPT-4's official release (2023-03-14). During the entire Sydney incident (mid-to-late February 2023), users were most likely conversing with GPT-3.5, not GPT-4.

### Performance and benchmarks

GPT-4 surpassed GPT-3.5 by wide margins across all major benchmarks:

| Benchmark | GPT-4 | GPT-3.5 | Improvement |
|------|:--:|:--:|:--:|
| Uniform Bar Exam | 90%ile | 10%ile | +80 |
| LSAT | 88%ile | 40%ile | +48 |
| SAT Evidence-Based Reading & Writing | 710/800 | 670/800 | +40 |
| GRE Quantitative | 163/170 | 147/170 | +16 |
| MMLU (common sense + reasoning) | 86.4% | 70.0% | +16.4 |
| HellaSwag (common-sense reasoning) | 95.3% | 85.5% | +9.8 |
| HumanEval (coding) | 67.0% | 48.1% | +18.9 |
| LeetCode (medium) | 31/41 | 12/41 | +19 |

On Codeforces programming contests, GPT-4 reached a rating of 392, surpassing the median human competitor.

### Why "that door was left unopened"

GPT-4's release triggered an interesting dual reaction:

On one hand, its capabilities were astonishing — surpassing average human performance on virtually every standardized exam, expressing fluency in multiple languages, understanding the visual world. This was unimaginable in the pre-GPT era.

On the other hand, it offered no hint of "the next step." GPT-2 → GPT-3 was a 100x leap in parameters; GPT-3 → GPT-4 disclosed no data measuring any such leap. External MoE speculation suggested a more pragmatic rather than grandiose scaling strategy — "distributing costs across multiple experts" rather than "packing all capabilities into one larger dense model."

This opacity later became a source of community dissatisfaction. In the period following GPT-4's release, multiple open-source teams (Meta, Mistral, Alibaba, DeepSeek) began pursuing at extraordinary speed — and one of their primary motivations was: "The things OpenAI won't tell us, we'll figure out ourselves."

### Impact

- At launch, GPT-4 was the best-performing AI model on benchmarks in the world, defining the "frontier" standard for 2023–2024
- Starting with GPT-4, "not disclosing parameters and training data" became the industry norm for closed-source large models
- GPT-4's multimodal capabilities (visual understanding) became a standard feature that all subsequent models would pursue — Claude 3, Gemini 1.0, and Llama 3.2 all incorporated visual input
- GPT-4-powered ChatGPT Plus subscriptions became a pillar of OpenAI's revenue, setting a precedent for the entire "model subscription" business model

## Claude: The birth of Constitutional AI

> A group who left OpenAI out of concern for AI safety founded a new company and built a model that was less skilled at poetry but far less likely to lie. Claude was not here to benchmark against GPT-4 — it was here to prove that "alignment" itself could be a product.

### Background: The exodus from OpenAI

**Late 2020** — A notable schism occurred within OpenAI. Vice President Dario Amodei and Safety Policy Director Daniela Amodei (siblings) led a group of employees to leave and founded **Anthropic**. The immediate catalyst was a disagreement over the prioritization of "safety": Dario and others believed OpenAI was underinvesting in safety under commercialization pressure.[^4]

The name "Anthropic" derives from the "anthropic principle," reflecting its core philosophy: AI systems should be centered on human interests. After its founding, the company received massive investment including from Google, and focused on "AI safety and alignment."

### Constitutional AI

Anthropic developed a unique training method called **Constitutional AI**.[^5] Unlike OpenAI's RLHF, Constitutional AI does not directly have human annotators evaluate outputs — instead, it first provides the model with a "constitution" (a set of explicit behavioral guidelines drawn from the UN Universal Declaration of Human Rights, Apple's Terms of Service, etc.), allowing the model to **evaluate and revise its own outputs**. The specific process:

1. Use the model's own evaluative capability to critique and revise responses to harmful prompts
2. Train a preference model from AI-generated feedback
3. Fine-tune the model with RL

The core philosophy of this approach is: **Don't use humans to judge what is good; let the model judge using rules.** The advantages: scalability (no need for massive human annotation), transparency (rules are publicly auditable), and auditability.

### Claude's release

**2022-12** — Anthropic internally deployed an initial version of Claude.
**2023-03-14** (the same day as GPT-4, coincidentally or deliberately) — Anthropic opened Claude to partners via API. Claude's positioning was clear: not a "smarter" ChatGPT, but a "more honest, more harmless" conversational AI.[^6]

Compared to ChatGPT, Claude exhibited several notable differences:
- More willing to admit ignorance — less prone to fabricating answers
- Lower threshold for refusing potentially harmful questions
- More conservative, academic output style — less "creative" than ChatGPT
- Weaker code-generation capabilities than GPT-4

### The divergence of two paths

GPT-4 and Claude were released on the same day (coincidentally or deliberately), which was itself a landmark fork in the road:

| | GPT-4 | Claude |
|---|---|---|
| Core philosophy | Stronger capabilities, aligned through post-training | Safety built in from the design stage |
| Safety method | RLHF (human feedback) | Constitutional AI (rule-based self-evaluation) |
| Openness | Closed-source, even parameters undisclosed | Closed-source, but methodology papers published |
| Market positioning | Strongest capability, all-purpose assistant | Honest, harmless, auditable |
| Pricing strategy | $20/month Plus | Enterprise-oriented, API usage |

This fork persists to this day — Claude 4 and GPT-5 in 2026 still represent two different AI design philosophies: one is "stronger capabilities + stronger guardrails," the other is "safe by design."

### Commentary

March 2023 was the most crowded month in large model history. GPT-4 and Claude launched on the same day, and Llama launched nine days earlier. Together, these three events sent a clear signal: **the era of large models had ended the "one dominant player" paradigm.**

GPT-4 defined the upper bound of "most powerful," Claude defined the signpost of "safest," and Llama defined the starting point of "what open source can do." Over the following two years, the tension among these three poles (capability / safety / openness) constituted the fundamental structure of the entire large model industry. When a company pursued GPT-4 in capability, it had to make choices about safety and openness — precisely the questions GPT-4 itself left unanswered.

GPT-4's true legacy was not its parameter count or benchmark scores, but its becoming a **yardstick** — not the benchmarks used to measure model quality, but a social benchmark for measuring "how far you are from the world's best." "This is GPT-4 level" — this phrase became standard parlance in the large model industry from 2023 to 2025. Every company claimed to have reached "GPT-4 level," yet GPT-4 itself was opaque — how could a model with unknown parameters and undisclosed architecture become "the benchmark"? This paradox perfectly illustrates that GPT-4's symbolic significance exceeded its technical significance: it was not a mountain that could be precisely measured, but a direction toward which everyone was running.

## Bard: Google's hasty response and a hundred-billion-dollar stumble

> After ChatGPT launched, Google's leadership declared a "Code Red." Four months later, Bard got a question about the Webb Telescope wrong in a Paris demo — and over $100 billion in Google's market capitalization evaporated. This was not a technical failure; it was a fear-driven launch disaster.

### Background: Code Red

Following ChatGPT's launch on November 30, 2022, Google's leadership responded swiftly. According to *The New York Times* on December 21, 2022, Google internally declared a "Code Red" — fearing ChatGPT threatened Google's core search business. Sundar Pichai summoned Larry Page and Sergey Brin back to the company for strategic discussions and mobilized multiple teams to accelerate AI product development.[^7]

Google was not lacking in AI technical expertise. It had invented the Transformer (2017), possessed the LaMDA conversational model (2021), and had long been deeply integrating machine learning into core products like Search, Translate, and Recommendations. But Google had been slow to release a conversational AI product to the public — the reasons included both safety concerns and the commercial straitjacket of "search ads are our core revenue, and conversational answers might cannibalize ad clicks." ChatGPT's emergence as a free chat product broke Google's rhythm: if you don't move, someone else will.

### Bard's launch and the demo debacle

**2023-02-06** — Sundar Pichai announced **Bard** on the official Google Blog, an experimental conversational AI product based on LaMDA. The blog stated that Bard would open to "trusted testers" in the coming weeks, followed by the public.[^8]

The critical context: just the day before (2023-02-05), Microsoft had announced it would hold a "surprise" AI event at its Redmond headquarters on February 7. The industry widely expected Microsoft to integrate ChatGPT into Bing. Google chose to announce Bard the day before Microsoft's event — the timing was no coincidence, but a panic-driven preemptive strike.

**2023-02-08** — At a launch event in Paris, Google demonstrated Bard's answering capabilities. In the live demo, Bard was asked: "What are the new discoveries from the James Webb Space Telescope (JWST)?" In its response, Bard stated that the JWST "took the very first images of a planet outside of our solar system."

In fact, the first image of an exoplanet was captured by the Very Large Telescope (VLT) in 2004, not the JWST. This was a factual error.

Google's stock price fell approximately 7.7% that day, erasing over $100 billion in market capitalization.[^9]

This stumble was more than a simple technical mishap. Google's advertising business relied on its brand image as a "trustworthy information provider"; an AI delivering a factual error before the world's eyes — and an easily verifiable one at that — inflicted immediate damage on that brand. Even more embarrassingly, Google was the world's largest search engine, yet its AI had gotten wrong a question that could have been verified using its own search engine.

### "Rushed" and internal criticism

Bard's launch drew widespread criticism from within Google.

According to CNBC, Google employees flooded the internal forum Memegen with complaints, calling the launch "rushed," "botched," and "un-Googley." One employee wrote: "Dear Sundar, the Bard launch and layoff announcement were rushed, botched, and short-sighted. Please return to the long-term roadmap."[^10]

This criticism pointed to a deeper problem. Google had just announced layoffs of approximately 12,000 employees in January 2023 (about 6% of its global workforce) — simultaneously cutting staff while rushing out a half-baked AI product. To employees, this signaled a loss of focus: What was the company actually fighting for? The future of AI, or short-term stock prices?

### Official launch

**2023-03-21** — Bard opened to the public in the US and UK, with users able to join a waitlist. Google deliberately adopted a low-key approach, positioning Bard as an "experiment" rather than a search replacement, emphasizing that users needed to verify information themselves.[^11]

Subsequent iterations:
- **2023-05-10**: At Google I/O, Bard was upgraded to PaLM 2, the waitlist was removed, and it launched in over 180 countries and regions
- **2023-07-13**: Bard launched in the EU (previously delayed by GDPR privacy reviews)
- **2023-12-06**: Google released **Gemini**, upgrading Bard to Gemini Pro
- **2024-02-08**: Bard was renamed **Gemini**, and the "Bard" brand was officially retired

### Commentary

Bard's launch was one of the most classic "panic launch" case studies in AI history. Google possessed virtually every technological first-mover advantage of the large model era — Transformer, TPU, LaMDA, DeepMind — but what truly tripped it up was not technical capability, but fear.

ChatGPT's explosive popularity made Google feel its core business was threatened. Driven by fear, it skipped internal safety reviews, failed to adequately test factual accuracy before the demo, and chose to preemptively launch the day before Microsoft's Bing event — every step driven by "don't fall behind" logic rather than "build a great product" logic. When the incorrect JWST answer appeared on the big screen, Google lost not just a demo, but public faith in the belief that "this company is still the leader in AI."

More ironically, a year later Google chose to abandon the "Bard" brand entirely, pivoting to Gemini. The name itself became a memory no one wanted to recall — a hastily launched product punished by the market and ultimately erased from the brand. But for the history of large models, Bard stands as a footnote to the first shockwave triggered by ChatGPT, and vivid evidence of how a tech titan can lose its footing during a paradigm shift.
