# September 2024

## o1: A new category of reasoning models

> OpenAI stopped pursuing "a bigger language model" and built "a language model that thinks." o1 performs chain-of-thought reasoning before answering — human "slow thinking" embedded in a commercial AI product for the first time. The category of reasoning models was born.

### Release

**2024-09-12** — OpenAI released **o1** (originally codenamed "Strawberry"), the first "reasoning model."[^1]

o1's core difference: before answering, it internally generates a **hidden chain of thought** — using additional reasoning time to plan, verify, and revise its thinking. Users see only the final answer; the intermediate reasoning process is deliberately hidden.

The design philosophy was clear: GPT-4's "next token" prediction is intuitive fast thinking — fluid and quick, but error-prone on tasks requiring multi-step reasoning. o1's "think before speaking" is deliberative slow thinking — slower, more computationally intensive, but far more accurate on mathematics, coding, and scientific reasoning.

### Performance

o1 achieved a leap in reasoning-intensive benchmarks:[^1]

| Benchmark | o1 | GPT-4o | Improvement |
|------|:--:|:--:|:--:|
| AIME 2024 (math competition) | 74.4% | 12.1% | **+62.3** |
| GPQA Diamond (scientific reasoning) | 77.3% | 56.1% | +21.2 |
| Codeforces | 89%ile | 11%ile | +78 |
| International Math Olympiad | 83.3% | 13.4% | +69.9 |

In the 2024 International Olympiad in Informatics (IOI), o1 scored 213 under human-equivalent conditions, ranking at the 49th percentile among all participants.

The most striking number was the AIME math competition: from 12% to 74%. This was not incremental improvement — it was a sudden migration of capability from one domain to another.

### The hidden chain of thought

o1's most distinctive design decision was the **hidden chain of thought.** OpenAI cited "safety" as the reason — if users could see the model's internal reasoning, they might learn to bypass safety restrictions. But critics argued this directly reversed OpenAI's earlier commitment to "transparency" — DeepSeek-R1 (2025-01), released four months later, open-sourced and fully disclosed its chain of thought, giving o1 a direct and resounding rebuke.

OpenAI enforced a "zero tolerance" ban on users who attempted to view the chain of thought — any attempt to see o1's thinking process through prompt injection or jailbreaking would result in account suspension. This further escalated community frustration — "you claim this is for safety, but you won't let anyone verify your safety measures."

### The reasoning model paradigm

o1 pioneered an entirely new model category — the "reasoning model." All prior LLMs (GPT series, Claude series, Llama series) operated in "direct generation" mode — predicting the next token without internal analytical steps. o1 proved a new scaling dimension: **inference-time compute.**

"Test-time compute" — having the model spend more time reasoning before answering — became the hottest research direction of 2024–2025. Subsequent models including DeepSeek-R1, Kimi K1.5, Claude extended thinking, and Qwen3-Thinker all ran on the track o1 had opened.

### From o1-preview to o1 final

- **2024-09-12**: o1-preview and o1-mini released, limited to ChatGPT Plus/Team and API Tier 5 users
- **2024-12-05**: o1 final version released, adding multimodal (image input) support
- **2024-12-20**: o3 released (skipping o2), setting new highs in coding and math

When o1-preview launched, it created a sensation, but users quickly discovered many issues: it over-thought simple problems ("A cat has 4 legs — how many legs do 10 cats have?" — thinking for 20 seconds), was less flexible than GPT-4o on certain conversational tasks, and its API pricing was extremely expensive (o1-preview output at $60 per million tokens, 4 times GPT-4o's rate).

### Impact

o1 marked the paradigm shift of large models from "bigger" to "smarter." It answered a question left open in the GPT-4 era: pure parameter scaling may be approaching diminishing returns, but **reasoning scaling** (having the model spend more time thinking) still had enormous room for improvement.

Over the following six months, virtually every AI company in the world released its own reasoning model — proving the viability of the "category" o1 defined. But it also proved that o1 could not hold its exclusive advantage — the technical barrier for reasoning models was lower than for pre-training models, and the catch-up was faster.

### Commentary

o1 marked the paradigm shift of large models from "bigger" to "smarter." It answered a question GPT-4 had left open: pure parameter scaling was approaching diminishing returns, but **reasoning scaling** (having the model spend more time thinking) still had enormous room for improvement. This was a new scaling law — not training-time compute, but inference-time compute.

The decision to hide the chain of thought was o1's most controversial aspect. OpenAI said this was for safety — but safety verification requires transparency, and concealment itself is opaque. Four months later, DeepSeek-R1 gave o1 its loudest answer: reasoning models can be both powerful and transparent, with MIT open-source and fully disclosed chain of thought. o1 pioneered the category, but ceded the opportunity to define its rules to DeepSeek.

From a product perspective, o1 also exposed a core tension in reasoning models: users do not necessarily want a "deliberative" AI. ChatGPT's appeal lies in instant conversation — as natural as chatting. o1's "wait 30 seconds for a better answer" is an interruption for everyday chat but a blessing for math problems. This tension remains unresolved — subsequent reasoning models have all attempted to "let fast thinking and slow thinking coexist," but no one has found an elegant UI to accommodate both simultaneously.

## o1 addendum: The Q* backstory, overthinking, and the price anchor

> The entry above has recorded o1's core facts. The following supplements three items: the rumor-laden backstory before o1's release, the underestimated product decision of o1-mini, and o1's pricing in the economic history of reasoning models.

### Q* and Strawberry: The pre-release rumor era

o1 did not appear out of nowhere. Before its official release, OpenAI internal codenames and external rumors had been brewing for nearly a year.

**November 2023** — On the eve of Sam Altman's brief dismissal by the OpenAI board, Reuters reported that an internal project called **Q\*** (Q-Star) had achieved a breakthrough. The report stated that several OpenAI employees had written to the board, warning that Q*'s progress might threaten humanity — this letter was reportedly one of the triggers for the board's decision to fire Altman. Q* was described as a model capable of solving elementary school math problems — sounds unremarkable, but in AI safety circles, "can solve math" was considered a critical milestone on the path to general reasoning.[^2]

In retrospect, Q* was very likely o1's predecessor. OpenAI never officially confirmed the relationship between the two, but the timeline aligns: the Q* rumors appeared in November 2023, and o1 was released 10 months later (2024-09-12).

In the summer of 2024, another codename — "Strawberry" — began appearing in OpenAI's job postings and oblique social media mentions. The Information reported in July 2024 that Strawberry was an OpenAI reasoning enhancement project capable of extended, multi-step reasoning.[^3] Two months later, o1 was released under the Strawberry codename — o1-preview's system prompts still contained traces of Strawberry.

This backstory matters not because it affected the model itself — but because it shaped **public expectations for o1.** When OpenAI released o1 in September 2024, the market had already been primed by a full year of "mysterious breakthrough" narratives. This was OpenAI's signature rhythm: let rumors ferment, echo them with a name (o1 = Strawberry), and finally deliver on part of the expectation with a demo. From GPT-2's "too dangerous to release all at once" to o1's Q* → Strawberry → o1, this narrative funnel had become OpenAI's standard playbook.

### o1-mini: Faster, cheaper, more focused

On o1's launch day, OpenAI also released **o1-mini** — a smaller, faster, cheaper reasoning model. o1-mini specialized in STEM (science, technology, engineering, and mathematics) reasoning, and while it underperformed o1-preview on non-STEM tasks, its cost-effectiveness on math and coding far exceeded the flagship's.

The pricing gap was significant:[^1]
- o1-preview: Input $15 per million tokens, Output $60 per million tokens
- o1-mini: Input $3 per million tokens, Output $12 per million tokens

o1-mini was 80% cheaper, yet on certain reasoning benchmarks its performance approached or even exceeded o1-preview. For most developers' practical scenarios (code generation, math problem-solving, data cleaning), o1-mini was the more rational choice.

This decision was elegant at the product level. o1-preview established the brand ceiling of "strongest reasoning," but its high pricing deterred most people; o1-mini served as a low-barrier entry point, letting developers try "reasoning models" at low cost. Together, they formed a dual-layer pricing structure of "brand elevation + volume" — the same logic as Mercedes-Benz's flagship S-Class and volume-selling C-Class.

### Overthinking: The first product challenge of reasoning models

o1's users quickly discovered a phenomenon that was equal parts amusing and frustrating: it would overthink when it shouldn't think at all.

"A cat has 4 legs — how many legs do 10 cats have?" — o1-preview would spend 20 seconds "thinking" about this question whose answer was obviously 40, generating hundreds of words of reasoning chain before giving the answer. In more extreme cases, users asking a simple "hello" would also trigger o1's reasoning mode, carefully analyzing the greeting's meaning and possible response strategies.

This was not a bug — it was the first systemic challenge in reasoning model productization. All prior language models operated in "fast thinking" mode: input in, output out. They made mistakes too, but their failure mode was intuitive — fast and careless. o1's slow thinking dramatically reduced error rates on complex tasks but wasted users' time and API costs on simple ones.

The problem this phenomenon exposed was deeper than it appeared: **reasoning models lack "task difficulty perception."** It does not know whether a question is 1+1=2 or an AIME competition problem — it simply generates reasoning chains following the patterns learned during training. And what training taught it was "all questions deserve a think."

Later reasoning models (Claude 3.7 Sonnet, Gemini 2.5 Pro, Qwen3) all attempted to address this — through "thinking toggles" that let users decide when slow thinking was needed. But this approach also had a cost: it shifted the judgment burden to users. Between o1's "always think" and Claude's "user decides," the industry has yet to find the perfect middle ground.

### The economics of pricing

o1-preview's output pricing — $60 per million tokens — was a landmark number in large model pricing history. GPT-4o's output was $15 per million tokens. o1 was 4 times more expensive.

The justification for this premium was not parameter count, but **inference-time compute.** o1 consumed far more tokens than ordinary models when answering questions — those hidden chains of thought were also billed by the token. Users were paying not only for the final answer but also for the "thinking process."

Thus o1's pricing created a new large model commodity form: **priced by the amount of thinking.** This was a first for the GPT series — GPT-3 and GPT-4's prices primarily reflected model scale and training cost, while o1's price reflected runtime computational consumption. Previous token billing charged for "generated content"; o1's token billing charged for "generated thinking" — thinking that users could not see.

This raised a trust issue: if I pay for thinking but cannot see the thinking process, how do I know my money went to genuine reasoning rather than wasted cycles? OpenAI's response was "trust us" — but with hidden chains of thought, that trust was hard to establish. DeepSeek-R1's later approach — disclosed chains of thought + extremely low pricing — was a direct response to this "hidden + expensive" model.
