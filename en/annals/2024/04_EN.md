# April 2024

## Llama 3: The first truly usable open-source model

> 15 trillion tokens, two sizes (8B / 70B), leading across benchmarks at comparable scale. This was not just another iteration of the Llama family — this was the first time an open-source large model truly crossed the threshold of "usable." Llama 2's reputation was "decent"; Llama 3's reputation was "genuinely good." The gap was not in parameters, but in training data and engineering detail.

### Release

**2024-04-18** — Meta released **Llama 3**, initially making model weights available in two sizes: 8B and 70B.[^1]

Llama 3's release continued Meta's open strategy since Llama 2: model weights were directly downloadable from Hugging Face and Meta's website, with commercial use permitted (subject to Meta's Community License Agreement). Also launched simultaneously was Meta AI — an AI conversational product built on Llama 3, integrated into Facebook, WhatsApp, Instagram, and meta.ai.[^2]

### The leap in training scale

Llama 3's core upgrade was not in architecture — it still used the standard Transformer decoder architecture — but in **training data scale and quality**:

- **Training data**: Approximately 15 trillion tokens, over seven times that of Llama 2
- **Vocabulary size**: 128K token tokenizer, with dramatically improved coverage of non-English languages (especially code and multilingual text)
- **Context window**: 8K tokens (subsequent versions expanded to 128K)
- **Training efficiency**: Meta claimed that at equivalent compute, Llama 3's training efficiency was approximately three times that of Llama 2 — attributed to improved parallelism strategies and data filtering pipelines

Meta stated in its blog that Llama 3 was the model they had invested the most training compute in, using two clusters each containing 16,384 H100 GPUs during training.[^1]

### Benchmark performance

Llama 3 70B's benchmark performance at release attracted widespread attention. Meta's published data showed that 70B surpassed competitors of equivalent scale across multiple mainstream benchmarks:[^1]

| Benchmark | Llama 3 8B | Llama 3 70B | Gemma 7B | Mistral 7B | Claude 3 Sonnet |
|------|:----------:|:-----------:|:--------:|:----------:|:---------------:|
| MMLU | 66.6% | 82.0% | 64.3% | 62.5% | 77.1% |
| HumanEval | 62.2% | 81.7% | 32.3% | 29.3% | 73.0% |
| GSM8K | 79.6% | 93.0% | 57.6% | 52.2% | 92.3% |

The 70B's 81.7% on HumanEval (code generation) was particularly striking — it meant open-source models had, for the first time, approached the closed-source frontier in coding capability. The 8B model also comprehensively surpassed Gemma 7B and Mistral 7B, establishing a new benchmark for small-scale models.

### Community reaction

Llama 3's release ignited the open-source community. Downloads on Hugging Face exceeded one million within the first week. Within days, the community produced a massive number of derivative versions: quantized versions (GGUF, GPTQ, AWQ), fine-tuned versions (for code, medical, legal, and other vertical scenarios), and optimized versions for consumer-grade hardware.[^3]

A landmark event: within 48 hours of release, users had already run Llama 3 8B local inference on a MacBook Pro using llama.cpp. The gap between "can run" and "useful" had been filled by Llama 3's 15 trillion tokens.

### Aftermath

Meta previewed Llama 3's roadmap: a larger 405B model (released in July 2024), multilingual and multimodal expansion, and longer context windows.[^1] Llama 3 405B was later released in July 2024 in its "complete form," becoming the largest open-source model at the time.

> 📖 See also: "The Llama Dynasty," "The GPT Dynasty," "Biography of GPT-4," "The Claude Dynasty," "Annals of Anthropic," "The DeepSeek Dynasty," "Annals of DeepSeek."

### Commentary

Llama 3 was not a technical breakthrough — its architecture contained no innovations. But it was a watershed in open-source model history, because it answered a critical question in the most straightforward way: **can open-source models actually be used?**

Before Llama 3, the answer was "sort of." Llama 2's real-world experience was far inferior to GPT-3.5 — it had clear gaps in reasoning, coding, and instruction following. If enterprises needed reliable AI capabilities, they still had to choose OpenAI or Anthropic's closed-source APIs. Llama 2's niche was "the free alternative" — better than nothing.

Llama 3 changed the landscape. 70B approached or surpassed GPT-4's same-scale competitors on mainstream benchmarks; 8B dominated in the small-scale category. More critically: these were not just benchmark numbers — community testing within days of release generally concluded that Llama 3 70B's conversational quality and instruction-following capability were "close to GPT-4." This was the first time an open-source model had earned the right to say that.

Meta's cost for this: 15 trillion tokens of training data and the compute of two 10,000-card clusters. This was not a small number. But it proved a logic: when training data and training engineering are pushed to the extreme, standard architectures can also produce frontier-level models. This logic was later repeatedly validated by DeepSeek in 2024–2025 — but Llama 3 was the first to prove it through action.

From an industry perspective, Llama 3 was Meta's smartest move in the AI race. Meta did not need to make money from APIs — its business model was advertising. Opening Llama 3's weights and letting the entire ecosystem build around Llama was eroding OpenAI and Google's moats. "If the best open-source model is Llama, then everyone — including cloud providers, startups, and researchers — is fighting Meta's war." This was not charity; this was strategy.

## The small model race: Phi-3, OpenELM, and "on-device LLMs"

> Not every battlefield was about who could go bigger. In April 2024, Microsoft and Apple respectively released small language models targeting mobile devices — Phi-3 (3.8B) and OpenELM (270M–3B). These models' significance lay not in benchmark scores, but in a signal: large models were trying to leave the data center and run on your phone.

### Phi-3-mini: Microsoft's small model ambitions

**2024-04** — Microsoft released **Phi-3-mini**, 3.8B parameters, MIT license.[^4]

Phi-3-mini was the third generation of the Phi series, positioned as a "GPT-3.5-level model that can run on a phone." The 3.8B parameter size meant it could be quantized to 4-bit for real-time inference on devices like the iPhone 15 Pro. Microsoft's marketing emphasized two selling points: small size and high quality. *The New York Times* considered this a key move by Microsoft in the small language model track; *WIRED* and Ars Technica noted that Phi-3-mini's capabilities were "approaching GPT-3.5."[^4]

Phi-3's training strategy continued the core philosophy of the Phi series: compensating for limited parameter count with carefully curated high-quality data. Microsoft called this "textbook-quality data" — training small models on structured, high-quality text (rather than coarse internet crawls) to achieve strong performance on reasoning and knowledge-intensive tasks.

Phi-3 later expanded to 7B and 14B versions, but the initial 3.8B had already established the narrative that "small models can also be good."

### OpenELM: Apple's open-source debut

**2024-04-24** — Apple published the **OpenELM** (Efficient Language Model) paper on arXiv. Eight model sizes, ranging from 270M to 3B parameters.[^5]

OpenELM's technical highlight was **layer-wise scaling** — unlike traditional Transformers where each layer has the same parameter count, OpenELM allocated different parameter counts to different layers, improving accuracy at the same total parameter budget. Apple claimed that at approximately 1B parameters, OpenELM achieved 2.36% higher accuracy than OLMo while requiring half the pre-training tokens.[^5]

More importantly, Apple open-sourced OpenELM's training code, training logs, multiple checkpoints, and pre-training configurations. This was rare in Apple's product lineup — Apple was known for its closedness, and OpenELM was one of its few public moves in the open-source AI space.

OpenELM's release was interpreted externally as Apple laying the groundwork for **Apple Intelligence** — two months later (June 2024 WWDC), Apple officially launched its on-device AI strategy, with OpenELM's technical accumulation forming part of its foundation.

### Commentary

Neither Phi-3 nor OpenELM was "stunning." A 3.8B model cannot compete with a 70B on capability ceilings. But what they represented was not a technical breakthrough — it was a signal of **battlefield migration.**

The large model narrative of the first half of 2024 was "who's bigger, who's stronger." GPT-4, Claude 3, Gemini 1.5, Llama 3 70B — all behemoths in the data center. But at the same time, a quieter trend was forming: for large models to truly proliferate, they had to be able to run on end devices. Not everyone had API credits, not every scenario could tolerate network latency, not every enterprise was willing to send data to the cloud.

Phi-3 proved that "small models can also have decent reasoning ability"; OpenELM proved that "Apple is serious about this." These two signals pointed toward a future: the endgame of large models is not infinite expansion, but bifurcation — one part continues to grow larger and stronger, another part grows smaller and faster, until everyone's phone has a sufficiently capable local AI.

This future was progressively realized with Apple Intelligence in June 2024 and Phi-3.5 in October 2024. But April's two papers were the first signposts pointing in that direction.

## Mistral AI's funding rumors and the rise of European AI

> In mid-April 2024, Reuters reported that French AI startup Mistral AI was negotiating a €500 million funding round, which would double its valuation to at least €5 billion. Microsoft had already taken a $16 million stake two months earlier (2024-02-26). This company, barely a year old, was using Europe as its base to challenge the large model landscape dominated by the US and China.

### Background

Mistral AI was founded in Paris in April 2023 by three French AI researchers — Arthur Mensch (formerly Google DeepMind), Guillaume Lample, and Timothée Lacroix (both formerly Meta). The company completed a €105 million seed round less than two months after founding, and its valuation reached over €2 billion four months later.[^6]

Mistral's strategy was a dual-track approach of "open weights + closed API": models like Mistral 7B and Mixtral 8x7B were released with Apache 2.0 open-weight licenses, while flagship models like Mistral Large were offered through closed APIs via Le Chat and Azure.

### The April 2024 funding rumors

**2024-04-16** — Reuters reported that Mistral AI was negotiating to raise approximately €500 million (about $540 million), which would double its valuation to at least €5 billion if successful.[^6]

Two months earlier (2024-02-26), Microsoft had announced a $16 million investment in Mistral AI and brought its models to the Azure cloud platform.[^6] This investment was interpreted externally as Microsoft's "second leg" beyond OpenAI — while $16 million was trivial compared to Microsoft's multi-billion-dollar investment in OpenAI, the symbolic significance of the Azure partnership was greater: it meant Microsoft was willing to promote two competing models on its own cloud platform.

Mistral ultimately completed a €600 million (approximately $645 million) funding round in June 2024, reaching a valuation of €5.8 billion.[^6]

### Commentary

Mistral's story was the emblematic narrative of European AI in 2024. A Paris-based company barely a year old, with a valuation approaching €6 billion, ranked fourth among global AI startups — behind only OpenAI, Anthropic, and xAI, and the highest-ranked outside the San Francisco Bay Area.

But more noteworthy was Mistral's niche selection. It did not attempt to beat GPT-4 on benchmarks — what it offered was "competitive, open-weight, European-made" AI models. Under the EU AI Act's regulatory environment, "European-made" was itself a differentiating selling point. The Azure partnership gave it a channel to reach global enterprise customers without building its own cloud platform.

Microsoft's $16 million investment in Mistral was a negligible amount, but the signal was clear: Microsoft was hedging. If OpenAI's strategy went awry, Mistral was Plan B. This "giant hedging" model later became the norm in 2024–2025 — Google invested in Anthropic, Amazon invested in Anthropic, NVIDIA invested in virtually all major AI companies. The large model competition was not just a contest between models, but a game between capital networks.
