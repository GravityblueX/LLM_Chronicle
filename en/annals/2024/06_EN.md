# June 2024

## Qwen 2/2.5: Alibaba's full-spectrum open-source coverage

> From 0.5B to 72B, all under Apache 2.0 open-source. Qwen 2/2.5 was not the strongest open-source model — but it had the broadest coverage. When you need a 0.5B model that runs on a phone and a 72B model that can compete with GPT-4, sharing the same architecture and the same training methodology — this was Alibaba's bet: the future of AI lies not in a single super model, but in a series.

### Release

**2024-06** — Alibaba Cloud released **Qwen 2**, comprising four sizes: 0.5B, 1.5B, 7B, and 72B, covering all scenarios from mobile to server. **2024-09** — **Qwen 2.5** was released, adding 3B, 14B, and 32B intermediate sizes, forming a complete spectrum from 0.5B to 72B.[^1]

Qwen 2.5 achieved 85.0% on MMLU — slightly below Llama 3.1 405B's 88.6%, but the 72B parameters were only one-sixth of 405B. On Chinese-language benchmarks, Qwen 2.5 comprehensively led Llama and Mistral models of comparable size.

Alibaba's open-source strategy was clear: **not pursuing "biggest and strongest," but "most complete and most flexible."** Developers could choose the size for their scenario — 0.5B for phones, 7B for edge devices, 72B for enterprise servers. This "full lineup" strategy was unique in the global open-source ecosystem. Meta's Llama series had narrower coverage (8B/70B/405B), and Mistral's was even narrower (7B/Mixtral/Large).

The Qwen series accumulated hundreds of millions of downloads on HuggingFace, making it the most influential Chinese model product in overseas open-source communities.

## Claude 3.5 Sonnet: The collective choice of programmers

> Anthropic did something clever: instead of upgrading Opus, they upgraded Sonnet. Claude 3.5 Sonnet, at a lower price than Opus, achieved near-equivalent or even stronger results. The Artifacts feature completely redefined the "AI conversation" interface — a single conversation, a complete application.

### Release

**2024-06-20** — Anthropic released **Claude 3.5 Sonnet**, skipping Opus to directly upgrade the mid-tier line.[^2]

Claude 3.5 Sonnet surpassed Claude 3 Opus on multiple benchmarks — at only one-third the price. This was a major victory for Anthropic in cost-effectiveness. More importantly, its performance on coding benchmarks (HumanEval 92.0%, SWE-bench 40.6%) significantly outpaced GPT-4o — rapidly making it the preferred AI coding assistant among programmers worldwide.

### Artifacts

The **Artifacts** feature, released alongside Claude 3.5 Sonnet, changed the interaction model of AI conversations. Previously, user-AI interaction was confined to the chat box — text in, text out. Artifacts allowed Claude to directly generate an independent content window within the same interface — which could be executable code, a React component (previewable), an SVG graphic (renderable), or a design document.

This feature may seem like a mere UI improvement, but its impact on AI products was profound: **"chat" was no longer the only interaction mode. AI could "build," not just "answer."** Artifacts was widely imitated by major AI products in the year that followed — ChatGPT Canvas, Gemini's interactive panel, and the shared Co-pilot workspace were all successors to Artifacts.

### Impact

Claude 3.5 Sonnet + Artifacts allowed Anthropic to build loyalty among developers — particularly in coding and prototyping scenarios. By 2025, Claude had become the default benchmark line for coding performance (SWE-bench) in large models — not because it was the strongest, but because developers **felt it worked best.**

### Commentary

Skipping Opus to upgrade Sonnet — this appeared to be a technical roadmap adjustment, but was in fact a clever brand signal.

At Claude 3's launch, Anthropic positioned Opus as the "strongest, most expensive" flagship. Following the natural momentum, the expected upgrade path would be Claude 3.5 Opus — bigger, more expensive, stronger. But Anthropic did not do this. It upgraded the mid-tier Sonnet, achieving near-equivalent or superior results at lower cost. This move conveyed two signals: first, "we don't rely on piling on parameters to improve the experience"; and second, "the most expensive model is not necessarily the best model."

Artifacts precisely reinforced the second signal. It was not about "making AI stronger" — it was about "making AI's output directly usable." A single conversation generating a React component that could be directly previewed, copied, and deployed — eliminating the multi-step switching of "copy code → paste into editor → run." This user experience insight was Anthropic's innovation — ChatGPT Canvas and Gemini's interactive panel were later followers.

But Claude 3.5 Sonnet's most enduring legacy was its reputation for coding capability. It proved that a vast gap can exist between benchmark scores and actual user perception — developers collectively chose a model that scored slightly lower on certain benchmarks but "felt better to use." When model capabilities converge, trust and experience become the core competitive differentiators.

## Kling: China's "Sora moment" for text-to-video

> OpenAI released the Sora demo in February 2024, stunning the world. But Sora never opened to the public. Four months later, Kuaishou released Kling — not a demo, not a waitlist, but a public product where anyone could register, enter a prompt, and generate video. China's AI industry seized the "first-mover advantage" in text-to-video for the first time.

### Release

**2024-06-06** — Kuaishou announced the text-to-video large model **Kling**, with public beta testing opening on **2024-06-10**. The model employed a 3D VAE + DiT (Diffusion Transformer) architecture, supporting video generation up to 2 minutes, 1080p resolution, 30fps, with free aspect ratio adjustment.[^3]

When Kling launched, OpenAI's Sora had been announced for four months but was still in an "invite-only creators" beta phase; overseas products like Runway Gen-2 and Pika 1.0 produced videos that were markedly inferior to the Sora demo in duration, resolution, and physical plausibility. Kling broke this deadlock — it could not only generate fluid long videos, but was also **the first large-scale text-to-video product available to the general public.**

Kuaishou's advantage lay in data: as one of China's largest short-video platforms, Kuaishou possessed massive volumes of high-quality video training data, encompassing various camera movements, scene transitions, human actions, and other visual patterns. Kling excelled in motion amplitude and physical plausibility (such as liquid splashing and object collisions), and in some evaluations was even considered to surpass the contemporary Runway Gen-3 Alpha.[^4]

### Iteration cadence

After launch, Kling maintained an extremely rapid iteration pace:

| Date | Version | Key updates |
|------|------|----------|
| 2024-06-06 | Kling 1.0 | Initial release, supporting 2-minute 1080p text-to-video |
| 2024-07 | Kling 1.0 image-to-video | Added image-to-video, supporting first/last frame control |
| 2024-09 | Kling 1.5 | Major improvements in visual quality and motion coherence |
| 2024-12 | Kling 1.6 | Further optimization of details and physics simulation |

### Industry impact

Kling's launch reshaped the competitive landscape of China's AI video track and altered external perceptions of "whether Chinese AI can do video generation":

- **Product before demo**: OpenAI used the Sora demo to establish the impression that "text-to-video = American leadership," but Kling was the first team globally to make high-quality text-to-video into a publicly usable product. This was not a technical surpassing, but a victory of product strategy — "usable" is more persuasive than "impressive but inaccessible."
- **Domestic competitors accelerated**: After Kling, ByteDance's Jimeng, Tencent Hunyuan Video, MiniMax Video-01, and other domestic text-to-video products launched in rapid succession, making the second half of 2024 an explosion window for Chinese AI video.
- **Open-source ecosystem stimulation**: While Kling did not open-source its model weights, its product success indirectly stimulated acceleration in open-source video generation models — CogVideoX (Zhipu), Open-Sora, and other open-source projects significantly quickened their iteration in the second half of 2024.

### Commentary

Kling's historical position does not lie in being technically superior to Sora — in fact, Sora's demo still led in visual quality. Its position lies in the **timing gap.**

When Sora released its demo in February 2024, the entire world thought the "ChatGPT moment" of text-to-video had arrived. But OpenAI chose not to release a product — safety reviews, compute costs, abuse risks, and various other considerations kept it from acting. This period of hesitation became a window for Chinese teams. Kuaishou did not wait: four months later, it launched directly into public beta, using "usable" to defeat "impressive."

Kling taught the industry one thing: in the large model era, first-mover advantage lies not in the flashiness of a demo, but in the speed of productization. While competitors are still polishing their demos, if you release first, the users, data, and feedback all become yours. This lesson was repeatedly validated afterward — DeepSeek-R1's rapid open-sourcing and Qwen's intensive iteration followed the same logic.

Kling also demonstrated the unique advantage of short-video platforms: video training data was not scraped from the internet but accumulated from a real creator ecosystem. This "data moat" runs deeper than model architecture.
