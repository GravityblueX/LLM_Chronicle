# May 2024

## GPT-4o: When AI learned to converse in real time

> Average latency of 320 milliseconds — comparable to human conversational response time. GPT-4o was not just multimodal; it was natively multimodal: text, speech, and vision shared the same neural network, no longer requiring "convert to text first, then process." Free for all users — this decision was as important as the model itself.

### Release

**2024-05-13** — OpenAI released **GPT-4o** ("o" for omni).[^1]

GPT-4o's core was not stronger benchmarks — its core was a **qualitative change in interaction modality**:
- **Native multimodality**: A single neural network simultaneously processing text, speech, and images, without needing to convert to text first like GPT-4
- **Real-time voice conversation**: Average response latency of 320ms (GPT-4's voice mode took 2.8–5.4 seconds) — approaching human conversational response speed
- **Emotional expression**: Capable of sensing emotion in the user's voice and responding with emotionally modulated tone
- **Real-time visual understanding**: Able to analyze the user's surroundings in real time through the camera

The launch demo showcased: GPT-4o describing the view outside a window for a blind person, translating a conversation between two people in real time, and guiding a user step-by-step through solving a handwritten math problem from an image. Each of these capabilities was not new individually, but integrating them into a "real-time conversation" experience produced a qualitative transformation.

### Model details

GPT-4o's specific parameter count and architecture were not disclosed. Known information:[^1]
- Matched or slightly outperformed GPT-4 Turbo across multiple benchmarks
- Dramatically improved non-English language capabilities — especially Chinese, Japanese, Korean, and Arabic
- Visual understanding capabilities approaching GPT-4V but with lower inference costs
- API pricing: Input $5 per million tokens, Output $15 per million tokens — 50% cheaper than GPT-4 Turbo

### The decision to go free

The most important announcement at GPT-4o's launch was not a technical upgrade, but a change in business model: **GPT-4o was made available to free users.** Previously, GPT-4 had been exclusive to the $20/month ChatGPT Plus subscription. GPT-4o broke down that paywall.

The logic behind this decision: OpenAI needed to maintain competitiveness at the free tier. Google's Gemini was already free, Meta's Llama 3 was open-source and free, and Claude had a free version — if OpenAI insisted on keeping GPT-4 behind a paywall, it would be eroded in user scale.

From a business perspective, the "free GPT-4o + Plus users get 5x usage limits" model proved successful — it sustained free user growth while preserving the conversion logic for paid users.

### Impact

GPT-4o redefined the interaction standard for "AI assistants." Voice conversation went from "ask a question, wait two seconds" to "as natural as talking to a person." It did not raise the capability ceiling like GPT-3 or GPT-4, but it made existing capabilities **useful for everyone.**

### Commentary

320 milliseconds. This was GPT-4o's most important technical metric. Not because it was technically difficult — but because it precisely crossed the "naturalness threshold" of human conversation. Latency exceeding half a second made it feel like "talking to a machine"; latency below half a second made it feel like "talking to a person." GPT-4o achieved the latter.

But GPT-4o's true historical significance lay in making it freely available. This was not a technical decision — it was a business decision. In 2024, OpenAI faced a dilemma: GPT-4 was only available to paying users, but Google's Gemini was free and Meta's Llama 3 was open-source and free. If OpenAI did not lower its paywall, it would be eroded in user scale. GPT-4o's free availability was the "first shot in the AI price war" — a war that later intensified with DeepSeek-V2 (2024-05) and the Chinese model price war of 2025, ultimately driving API prices down by over 99%.

From a product perspective, GPT-4o was OpenAI's redefinition of "what AI really is." Not "a stronger model" — but "more natural interaction." Real-time voice, emotion sensing, real-time visual understanding — these were not benchmark numbers, but qualitative shifts in user experience. This pivot later influenced the entire industry: Claude's Artifacts, Gemini's real-time vision, ChatGPT's Advanced Voice Mode — all competing within the same coordinate system: not who is smarter, but who is more natural.

## DeepSeek-V2: The MLA architecture and the spark of a price war

> 236B parameters, only 21B activated. API pricing at 1% of GPT-4. DeepSeek-V2 did not claim to beat GPT-4 — it simply used a new attention mechanism (MLA) and a massive price cut to tell everyone: frontier capability does not require frontier pricing.

### Release

**2024-05-06** — DeepSeek released **DeepSeek-V2**. A MoE architecture with 236B total parameters and 21B active parameters. Performance approached GPT-4 (MMLU 80.3%), with API pricing at approximately **1%** of GPT-4 Turbo — input ¥1 per million tokens, output ¥2 per million tokens.[^2]

DeepSeek-V2's primary technical contribution was architectural — it proposed **MLA (Multi-head Latent Attention)**, a method that dramatically compresses the KV Cache during inference. Traditional multi-head attention requires caching Key and Value matrices for every token, with KV Cache growing linearly with sequence length. MLA projects the KV into a low-dimensional latent space and reconstructs from it — maintaining attention quality while compressing KV Cache to 5%–10% of its original size.

This technique may appear to be an unremarkable engineering optimization. But its consequences were revolutionary: KV Cache was the biggest bottleneck in inference cost. Compressing it by 90% meant compressing inference cost by 90%. DeepSeek-V2's extremely low pricing was not money-burning — it was powered by MLA.

### Price war

DeepSeek-V2's pricing triggered a genuine price war in the Chinese AI community. The prior consensus was: GPT-4-class models should command GPT-4-class prices. DeepSeek broke this understanding:

- Alibaba Cloud quickly slashed Tongyi Qianwen API prices
- Baidu's ERNIE Bot followed suit
- Zhipu lowered GLM series API prices
- ByteDance's Doubao pushed prices even lower

Within less than a month, Chinese AI API prices had fallen to less than one-tenth of their levels at the start of the year. The community called it "DeepSeek the price butcher." Ironically: DeepSeek was not a commercial company waging a price war — its backer, High-Flyer Quant, was a quantitative hedge fund. It did not need to make money from APIs.

### Impact

DeepSeek-V2's most important legacy was not benchmark scores, but a single statement: **"GPT-4-class models don't require GPT-4-class budgets."** This statement was amplified to its extreme six months later with DeepSeek-R1 — but V2 was the starting point. MLA was also inherited by DeepSeek-V3 (2024-12) and R1 (2025-01), becoming DeepSeek's signature architecture.

### Commentary

V2 was one of the most underrated models in large model history. R1 captured the world's attention, and NVIDIA lost $589 billion in a single day — but R1 would have been impossible without V2. The MLA architecture cut inference costs to one-tenth — this engineering breakthrough was no less significant than any SOTA benchmark.

The deeper question: Was V2's pricing a business strategy or a technical inevitability? The answer was the latter. MLA was not money-burning subsidies — it structurally reduced inference costs. This meant DeepSeek's pricing advantage was not a temporary promotion, but a sustainable competitive advantage. When Alibaba, Baidu, Zhipu, and ByteDance followed with price cuts, they were matching DeepSeek's prices — but their cost structures had not changed. This dual advantage of "price leadership + cost leadership" was later validated for repeatability in V3 (training cost of only ~$5.6M).

V2 also revealed a harsher truth: if you don't need to make money from APIs — say, because you're a quantitative fund — then "low pricing" itself becomes a weapon. High-Flyer Quant did not need DeepSeek API revenue to survive; it could crush all commercially dependent model companies with extreme pricing. This was not market competition — this was "a wolf that doesn't need to eat entering an ecosystem of sheep that do." R1's MIT open-source release in 2025 pushed this logic even further — but V2 was where it began.
