# July 2024

## Llama 3.1 405B: Open source challenges GPT-4 head-on

> A 405B-parameter open-source model, for the first time benchmarking head-to-head with GPT-4. Zuckerberg simultaneously published an "open-source manifesto" — calling on the entire industry to embrace open-source AI. From leak incident to open-source manifesto, Meta took 16 months.

### Release

**2024-07-23** — Meta released **Llama 3.1**, with the flagship at **405B** parameters — the largest open-source model by parameter count ever created.[^1]

Llama 3.1 achieved parity with or approached GPT-4 and Claude 3.5 Sonnet across multiple benchmarks. It scored 88.6% on MMLU (GPT-4 scored 86.4%), 89.0% on the HumanEval coding benchmark, and 73.8% on MATH (GPT-4 scored 76.6%).

Technical details:
- Training data: 15T tokens (Llama 3 series)
- Context window: 128K tokens — matching GPT-4 Turbo
- Support for multilingual, tool calling, and structured outputs
- Training conducted on 16,000+ H100 GPUs

Smaller versions (8B and 70B) were also updated simultaneously, setting new open-source model benchmarks at every scale.

### Zuckerberg's "open-source manifesto"

Concurrent with Llama 3.1's release, Mark Zuckerberg published a blog post titled "Open Source AI Is the Path Forward."[^2] Core arguments:

1. AI should not be controlled by a handful of closed-source companies — it should become a public infrastructure for the entire industry, like Linux
2. Open-source AI is safer — because more people can audit it, fix it, and improve it
3. Meta open-sourcing Llama is not charity — it is strategy. A thriving open-source ecosystem erodes competitors' moats

This article sparked widespread debate in the AI industry. Supporters viewed Meta as the "guardian of open-source AI"; opponents argued that a social network giant should not define what "safety" means. Regardless, it was the highest-profile and most influential manifesto the open-source AI movement had produced to date.

### Impact

Llama 3.1 405B validated a critical assumption: **open-source models can approach closed-source frontier capability.** While GPT-4o still led in multimodality and voice interaction, in pure text capability, the 405B Llama 3.1 had crossed the threshold of "replacing closed source."

This also sparked controversy over "what does open source really mean." Llama 3.1's license was not OSI-certified open source — it restricted scale (entities with over 700 million monthly active users needed additional permission) and did not disclose training data. OSI (Open Source Initiative) later published the formal definition of open-source AI (OSAID), excluding Llama from "true open-source AI."

### Commentary

405B, open-source, benchmarking against GPT-4. Llama 3.1 was Meta's formal declaration to the world: closed-source advantages are not permanent. Zuckerberg's "open-source manifesto" was a strategic document so clever it bordered on cold-blooded — he wrapped Meta's commercial interests in the "moral obligation of open AI."

But the unspoken facts in the manifesto were equally important: Llama was not "truly open source." No disclosed training data, attached commercial restrictions — OSI later explicitly excluded Llama from the definition of open-source AI. What Meta built was not "open-source AI" but "open-weight AI." The difference: true open source lets you understand *why* the model works the way it does; open weights only let you use the model's output. The former is science; the latter is a product distribution strategy.

From an industry perspective, Llama 3.1 reaching GPT-4-level capability was a historical moment — not just technically, but psychologically. It killed the narrative that "open source is always one generation behind closed source." Subsequent Chinese open-source models (DeepSeek V3, Qwen 2.5), European open-source models (Mistral Large 2), and American open-source models (later Llama versions) all advanced on the same track. 405B was not the destination — it was the point that proved "this path works."
