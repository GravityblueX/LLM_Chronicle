# September 2019

## Megatron-LM: 8.3B, and the Dawn of Parallel Training

> NVIDIA trained the largest Transformer language model of its time — 8.3B parameters — using 512 GPUs. More important than the model itself was the method it open-sourced: model parallelism + data parallelism, transforming "large model training" from a trade secret of a few tech giants into an engineering problem that any practitioner could tackle.

### Release

**2019-09** — NVIDIA released **Megatron-LM** with 8.3B parameters. By this point, GPT-2 (1.5B, February 2019) had been out for 7 months — Megatron-LM was not the first large-parameter model, but it brought something far more important: an efficient model-parallel training method. The paper's core contribution was splitting Transformer attention heads and fully connected layers across multiple GPUs, combined with data parallelism, achieving near-linear scaling efficiency and turning "training billion-parameter models" from a closely guarded secret into a replicable engineering practice.[^1]

The 8.3B Megatron-LM was trained on 512 NVIDIA V100 GPUs. While the parameter count seems modest by today's standards, in 2019 it served as an engineering proof-of-concept — demonstrating that "as long as the parallelism strategy is right, adding more GPUs means adding more parameters." Megatron later became a core tool for training models at the scale of GPT-3.

### Impact

Megatron-LM inaugurated the subfield of "large-scale training infrastructure." Subsequent systems such as Megatron-Turing NLG (530B), DeepSpeed, FSDP, and others all continued along this trajectory. Its open-source code enabled small and mid-sized teams to experiment with training large models, lowering the engineering barrier to scaling.

### Commentary

Megatron-LM was not the kind of model that "changed the direction of AI" — but it belongs to another category of equally important contribution: making it engineering-feasible to "change direction" in the first place. Before Megatron, training billion-parameter models required each team to independently work out parallelism strategies, solve memory allocation problems, and write communication primitives from scratch. Megatron abstracted these challenges into reusable modules: splitting attention heads, splitting fully connected layers, overlapping computation with communication. This methodology was later absorbed and extended by GPT-3, DeepSpeed, and FSDP, becoming the engineering foundation of large model training.

NVIDIA, as a GPU hardware vendor, had an obvious commercial motive for releasing such an open-source training framework — lowering the training barrier meant more people buying GPUs. But objectively, Megatron's code and technical report had a more lasting impact than the 8.3B model it trained. This is a rare paper where "the method was worth more than the result."
