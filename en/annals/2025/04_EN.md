# April 2025

## Llama 4: The "Gaming the System" Controversy of the Open-Source Flagbearer

> Meta released the first MoE-architecture Llama. But the community was not celebrating — they were arguing. Meta had submitted a "specially optimized experimental version" to LMArena to game the rankings, while the actual open-source version was noticeably weaker. The "open-source flagbearer's" credibility was damaged overnight.

### Release

**2025-04-05** — Meta AI released **Llama 4**, comprising two versions:

| Version | Parameters | Architecture | Positioning |
|------|------|------|------|
| **Scout** | 109B (total), 17B (active) | MoE, 16 experts | Long context (10M tokens), runs on a single H100 |
| **Maverick** | 400B (total), 17B (active) | MoE, 128 experts | GPT-4o-class |

This was the Llama series' first adoption of **MoE (Mixture of Experts)** architecture — a shift from the dense Transformer decoders of Llama 1/2/3 to sparse activation. Of Maverick's 128 experts, only approximately 17B parameters are activated per inference pass, keeping inference costs under control. [^1]

Llama 4's Scout version deserves special mention: a 10M token context window — ten times that of Gemini 1.5 Pro. This was Meta's direct response to Google's ultra-long context advantage.

### Benchmarking controversy

Llama 4's release was quickly overshadowed by a benchmarking controversy. Meta submitted the Maverick version to LMArena, where it initially ranked extremely high — close to Gemini 2.5 Pro. But the community quickly discovered that Meta had submitted a **specially optimized "experimental version"**, not the final open-source version. When the actual open-source version was independently tested, its performance was noticeably lower than the LMArena scores. [^2]

This was not the first time a company had been accused of "gaming the system" on benchmarks — but it stung especially coming from Meta. Zuckerberg had just delivered an "open-source manifesto" during the Llama 3.1 release (July 2024), positioning himself as the AI industry's "open-source guardian." When the guardian itself began manipulating benchmark data, the credibility of the entire open-source movement was affected.

Meta's response was that the LMArena version was an "actively optimized development build" and that the open-source weights would continue to improve over time. But the community was not convinced — "if what you submitted isn't what you're open-sourcing, then you're gaming the rankings."

### Impact

Llama 4's release inflicted real damage on Meta's open-source image. Although the technology itself (first open-source MoE, 10M context) was solid, the benchmarking controversy eclipsed the technical innovation. Against the backdrop of R1 winning community trust through MIT open-source and fully public chains of thought, Llama 4's "gaming the system" was particularly glaring.

The more far-reaching impact was that Llama 4 marked the moment when the "flagship of open-source LLMs" quietly slipped from Meta's grasp. DeepSeek-R1 (January 2025) and Qwen 3 (released days later) had already surpassed the Llama series in community trust.

### Commentary

The lesson of Llama 4 lies not in MoE, nor in long context, but in trust. The competition among open-source models has never been solely about parameters and scores — it is also about promises and verifiability. Meta can explain the distinctions between experimental versions, development builds, and ongoing optimizations, but what the community actually heard was: what's on the leaderboard is not the same as what you get in your hands.

When a project positions itself as the "open-source flagbearer," it faces more rigorous scrutiny than closed-source companies — because the value of open-source is built precisely on transparency. Llama 4 remains an important technical milestone, yet it is also a reputational fault line: the stratum has not fully collapsed, but the cracks are clearly visible.

## o3: Diminishing Returns for Reasoning Models

> o3 set new highs across benchmarks. But nobody was as excited as when o1 launched. Because the industry had come to understand: reasoning model capabilities are still growing, but the trajectory is shifting from "breakthroughs" to "optimization."

### Release

**2025-04-16** — OpenAI officially released and made available **o3** and **o4-mini**; o3 is the direct successor to the o1 reasoning model. This should be distinguished from the "o3 initial announcement/preview" on 2024-12-20: the end of 2024 was an announcement, while April 2025 was the official production release. [^3]

o3 continued o1's chain-of-thought approach — conducting undisclosed internal reasoning before responding. Key numbers:

- AIME 2025 (math competition): **~91%** (o1 scored 74.4%)
- Codeforces (coding competition): **~95th percentile** (o1 scored 89th percentile)
- SWE-bench: **~71%** (GPT-4o scored ~33%, Claude 3.5 Sonnet scored 40.6%)

But o3's pricing problem became even more pronounced — $60/million output tokens, four times GPT-4o's and hundreds of times Qwen 3's (released days later under Apache 2.0). With the capability gap insufficient to justify the price gap, o3's user base was extremely limited.

### Why no one was excited anymore

When o1 launched (September 2024), it was an entirely new category — "reasoning model." When o3 launched (April 2025), it was simply a better reasoning model. The excitement gap between the two was not a capability issue — it was a category issue. Once a category already exists, catch-up players have appeared (R1, Claude 3.7, Gemini 2.5), and open-source versions are imminent (Qwen 3), the "first-generation" halo has already faded.

### Commentary

o3 signaled the "maturation" of the reasoning model approach — and also "slowing growth." From GPT-4o to o1, AIME jumped from 12% to 74% — that was a category breakthrough. From o1 to o3, AIME jumped from 74% to 91% — that was optimization. The former changed perceptions of "what models can do." The latter merely improved on what was already known to be possible.

But o3's most awkward aspect was not technical — it was timing. It chose to launch in April 2025 — squeezed between Llama 4 (benchmarking controversy), Qwen 3 (full open-source + hybrid reasoning), and the upcoming Claude 4. In this crowded launch window, o3 was drowned out by noise. It was not unimpressive — but "impressive" alone was no longer sufficient at this level of competitive density.

## Qwen 3: Hybrid Reasoning + Full-Spectrum Open Source

> Alibaba simultaneously accomplished three things: released the strongest Qwen flagship (235B MoE), supported "thinking/non-thinking" dual-mode reasoning, and covered the full spectrum from 0.6B to 235B. All under the Apache 2.0 license. This was the most complete answer to "what open-source AI should look like."

### Release

**2025-04-29** — Alibaba Cloud released **Qwen 3** under the Apache 2.0 license. Full-spectrum coverage spanning 0.6B, 1.7B, 4B, 8B, 14B, 32B, 72B, 235B (MoE, 22B active). [^4]

Qwen 3's flagship **235B MoE** benchmarked against GPT-4o across multiple evaluations. It achieved approximately 80% on the AIME 2025 mathematics benchmark. The key innovation was the **"thinking/non-thinking" dual mode** — inspired by Claude 3.7 Sonnet, users could choose to have the model respond instantly to simple questions or enter reasoning mode for complex ones.

This was the second mainstream model after Claude 3.7 Sonnet to adopt a hybrid reasoning strategy — and the first to do so as open source.

### Why Qwen 3 was more popular than Llama 4

The two releases were only 24 days apart. While Llama 4 was mired in benchmarking controversy, Qwen 3 entered the market with a posture of "fully open-source, no tricks, full-spectrum coverage." The community's reaction was a stark contrast: Llama 4 was criticized; Qwen 3 was praised.

This reflected a larger trend: in 2025, Chinese open-source models were shifting from "catching up" to "surpassing." DeepSeek-R1 (January 2025) proved with MIT open-source that reasoning models could be open; Qwen 3 (April 2025) proved that open-source could be more flexible than closed-source with full-spectrum coverage. Meta had held the "open-source flagbearer" position for two years — now it had been dethroned.

### Impact

Qwen 3 made two contributions: technical hybrid reasoning (the first "thinking toggle" in an open-source model) and ecological full-spectrum coverage (a corresponding model for every node from phones to servers). It represented the highest standard for open-source LLMs in 2025 — not "a very strong model" but "a complete series."

### Commentary

April 2025 was the month the "open-source baton" was passed. Llama 4's benchmarking controversy and Qwen 3's full-spectrum open source formed two sides of a single narrative. One side was "the former flagbearer taking shortcuts"; the other was "a new competitor seizing the baton with sincerity."

These three events — Llama 4, o3, Qwen 3 — overlapped to paint the full panorama of April 2025:
- The closed-source frontier (o3) continued to optimize, but excitement was fading
- The open-source flagbearer (Llama) was losing trust
- China's open-source force (Qwen) was establishing standards

This was not a month's worth of news — it was a signal of an era's turning. Pre-training scaling had ended, reasoning scaling was maturing, the gap between open-source and closed-source was narrowing, and competition was shifting from "who is stronger" to "who is cleaner."
