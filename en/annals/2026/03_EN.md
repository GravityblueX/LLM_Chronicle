# March 2026

## Distillation Attacks, the "March 18 Incident," and the Rise of Domestic Models

> The Big Three closed a door; domestic models opened a window. This time, Chinese users were not conquered by technology but pushed toward domestic models by geopolitical forces — and then discovered they no longer needed anyone's mercy.

### Prelude: Distillation attacks (February 2026)

**2025-09** — Anthropic updated its terms of service, explicitly designating China as a restricted region. Enterprises with more than 50% Chinese ownership were prohibited from using Claude. The rationale was "U.S. national security policy — preventing advanced AI capabilities from being used for military and intelligence purposes." AtomGit, 2026-03-29[^1] (Anthropic's official blog did not mention the specific date of the terms update; 2025-09 is sourced from AtomGit's citation.)

**2026-02-23** — Anthropic publicly alleged on its official blog that three Chinese AI companies — **DeepSeek**, **Moonshot AI**, and **MiniMax** — had conducted "industrial-scale model distillation attacks" by using approximately 24,000 fake accounts to engage in over 16 million conversation exchanges with Claude. Anthropic claimed these actions were intended to extract Claude's core capabilities to train rival models and escalated the matter to the level of "national security." Anthropic Blog, 2026-02-23[^2]; DeepTech / Zhihu, 2026-02-25[^3]

These allegations triggered widespread mockery across the internet — Anthropic itself had just settled a copyright lawsuit in September 2025 for $1.5 billion, arising from downloading over 7 million books from pirated websites to train Claude, and simultaneously faced a $3 billion music copyright infringement claim (caveat: both figures appear only in the Zhihu source[^3] and no independent cross-verification has been found). Elon Musk satirized Anthropic on X as "MisAnthropic," writing: "How dare they steal what Anthropic stole from human programmers?" The Register's headline was even more direct: *Anthropic accuses Chinese AI labs of stealing content — just like it did*. DeepTech / Zhihu, 2026-02-25[^3] (Note: the Zhihu link may return a 403 due to anti-scraping measures and requires browser access.)

Notably, the timing of Anthropic's statement closely coincided with Pentagon pressure — according to Axios and Politico reports (cited via [^3], not independently verified), in mid-February 2026, the Pentagon issued Anthropic an ultimatum demanding the removal of Claude's safety restrictions for unrestricted military use by February 27. Analysts suggested that Anthropic's move was intended to leverage the geopolitical narrative to gain negotiating room. DeepTech / Zhihu, 2026-02-25[^3]

### The "March 18 Incident": Collective banning by the Big Three (March 16–18, 2026)

**2026-03-06** — A small wave of Anthropic account suspensions began. 80aj.com reported: "In March 2026, the Chinese community once again faced a wave of Anthropic/Claude account suspensions." (Note: the 80aj.com article URL and snapshot are problematic — index.json records `https://www.80aj.com/AI/20260306/` while the article text cites `https://www.80aj.com/2026/03/06/anthropic-account-suspension-2026/`; the two URLs are inconsistent and the snapshot content is an unrelated article, requiring re-archiving.)[^4]

**2026-03-16—18** — The risk control escalation triggered by distillation attacks culminated. Anthropic upgraded its risk control strategy from "IP-only" to "analyzing request patterns" — high-volume conversations in short periods, highly similar request structures, and frequent account switching all triggered bans. **OpenAI and Google simultaneously tightened API restrictions on the China region**, and relay stations and proxy services failed en masse. The community dubbed this event the **"March 16/17/18 Incident."**

The scope of this ban wave was extremely broad: not only were distillation attackers banned, but large numbers of ordinary Chinese individual users who used proxy services legitimately were also caught in the crossfire due to behavioral patterns that "collided" with distillation traffic. AtomGit summarized: "Those 24,000 fake accounts dirtied the entire road. Anthropic tightened the net too much to block distillation, and you got caught in it." AtomGit, 2026-03-29[^1]

**2026-03-29** — AtomGit (an open-source community under CSDN) published "Mastermind Exposed: 24,000 Fake Accounts, 16 Million Conversations — They Narrowed Claude's Path," the first complete analysis of the causal chain behind the ban wave: Anthropic restricts China in September 2025 → distillation attack exposed in February 2026 → risk control maxed out → individual users caught in the crossfire. [^1]

### Aftermath: Domestic models' response

The direct consequence of the March 18 Incident was the Chinese developer community's accelerated pivot toward domestic models. This response materialized in April 2026 — including further tightening of Claude's access from China, the launch of Zhipu's GLM-5.1 on the Modal platform, and the release of DeepSeek V4. See *Annals, April 2026*.

### Commentary

The "March 18 Incident" was a rare instance in large language model history of an ecosystem realignment directly triggered by access policies rather than technological breakthroughs.

Distillation attacks were technically unremarkable — Hinton proposed knowledge distillation in 2015, and the entire AI industry has been using it. [^5] Anthropic itself distills its own models to create smaller versions, and implicitly accepts that third parties can use API outputs to improve their own products — until this was done at industrial scale by Chinese companies. The real irony of the allegations was this: Anthropic used a model trained on 7 million books downloaded from pirated book websites to accuse others of "stealing" that model's outputs. Musk's comment was harsh but precise: "How dare they steal what Anthropic stole from human programmers?"

But regardless of whether the allegations were hypocritical, their consequences were real. Multiple overseas model services were reported by Chinese users as having unstable access within a similar timeframe, creating short-term migration pressure for Chinese developers who relied on overseas APIs or proxy services. However, what followed surprised everyone: domestic models were already there — no mercy needed, no patriotic "domestic substitution" sentiment required — they simply worked well, and were cheaper by two orders of magnitude.

This event also changed the collective psychology of China's AI community. Before this, "domestic models" still carried the undertone of "follower," and users often chose them out of necessity or patriotism. After the March 18 Incident, this narrative was upended — not "we can only use our own," but "our own are already excellent; it was you who forced us to discover it." The leap from "forced substitution" to "genuinely preferred" was not technological — it was cognitive.

At a deeper level, the distillation attack controversy exposed a fundamental contradiction of the large language model era: training data is a public resource (the internet), model capabilities are private property (APIs), and knowledge distillation technically blurs this boundary. Anthropic attempted to draw the line using "national security," but once that line is drawn, it severs not only the flow of technology but also the trust foundation of the global AI ecosystem. The long-term impact of the March 18 Incident would not fully manifest until DeepSeek V4's release the following month.

## LeWorldModel: A Universal Regularization Method for Embedding Prediction

> A few dozen random projection directions plus a normality test can replace all the patches of EMA, stop-gradient, and pretrained encoders. A good regularization term is worth more than a pile of heuristic tricks.

**2026-03** — Yann LeCun's team (Mila, NYU, Samsung SAIL, Brown University) released **LeWorldModel (LeWM)**, an end-to-end world model trained from raw pixels. Its most extractable contribution lies not in the specific architecture but in providing a concise, stable, and mathematically elegant regularization method for **embedding prediction loss functions**. arXiv:2603.19312[^6]

This core method is called **SIGReg** (Sketched Isotropic Gaussian Regularization). The idea is almost naively simple: when training an embedding prediction model, the latent embeddings output by the encoder are prone to **representation collapse** — mapping all inputs to the same constant vector, driving prediction error to zero, while the model learns nothing about the underlying physical laws. Previous researchers had used a battery of heuristic tricks to patch this vulnerability: EMA (exponential moving average), stop-gradient, self-distillation, frozen pretrained encoders — each a patch, collectively inflating hyperparameters to more than 6.

SIGReg solved this problem with a mathematically clean approach. It is based on the **Cramér-Wold theorem** — matching all one-dimensional marginal distributions is equivalent to matching the full joint distribution. The specific procedure: project high-dimensional embeddings along M random directions onto one dimension, apply the **Epps-Pulley normality test** to each projection, and aggregate the statistics as a regularization penalty. If the model attempts to collapse, the distribution must deviate from isotropic Gaussian, triggering a higher penalty; to minimize the penalty, the model is forced to spread embeddings uniformly across the entire high-dimensional space. The entire process requires no EMA, no stop-gradient, and no pretrained encoder.

The results are formidable: only ~15M parameters, trainable on a single GPU in hours; **hyperparameters reduced from 6+ to just 1** (regularization weight λ); planning speed 48x faster than DINOv2-based world models; surpassing previous end-to-end methods by 18 percentage points on the Push-T task, even exceeding proprioception-informed baselines (using pixels alone).

**Significance**: LeWM's specific architecture (ViT-Tiny encoder + AdaLN action injection + autoregressive predictor) may not be the final form, but the SIGReg regularization approach is transferable — for any loss function that "predicts the next embedding," simply adding this single regularization term can dramatically reduce the risk of training collapse. It transformed an engineering problem into a mathematical problem and replaced a pile of "try-and-see" patches with a "provable" guarantee. It is a minimalist template for loss design in embedding prediction.
