# AI Safety and Alignment: From Technical Solutions to Governance Games

> Every leap in large language model capability simultaneously produces two things: excitement and fear. Excitement comes from "what it can do"; fear comes from "will it do something it shouldn't." AI safety and alignment represent humanity's ongoing effort to manage that fear — from the earliest RLHF teaching models to "listen to humans," to Constitutional AI replacing human feedback with rules, to governments attempting to draw hard boundaries around models with legislation. This is not a straight line but a series of tug-of-war contests.

## I. RLHF: Pouring Human Preferences into Models

The safety alignment problem for large language models was first raised in a rather straightforward way: models are powerful, but they don't necessarily do what you tell them.

GPT-3 could write fluent essays, but it could also generate racist content, gender bias, and violent material. It wasn't intentional — it had simply seen these things in training data and faithfully reproduced them. Pre-training taught models to "speak," but it didn't teach them "what not to say."

**InstructGPT** was published by OpenAI in January 2022 under the title "Training Language Models to Follow Instructions with Human Feedback." It proposed a three-step process that would become the industry standard: first, collect human preference annotation data (human annotators rank multiple responses to the same prompt), then train a reward model on these preferences, and finally use reinforcement learning (the PPO algorithm) to adjust the language model so its generated responses better align with human preferences.[^1]

This process became known as RLHF (Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback). It did more than just "filter profanity." It taught models something far more nuanced: what constitutes a "good answer." Human annotators didn't just tell the model "you can't say this" — they taught it "this response is more helpful, more honest, and more harmless than that one."

Almost simultaneously, Anthropic published "Training a Helpful and Harmless Assistant with Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback." The paper explored the tension between Helpful and Harmless — sometimes the information a user wants may itself be harmful, and the model needs to find a balance between the two.[^2]

RLHF occupies a unique place in the history of large language models. It wasn't a technology that made models "smarter" — it was one that made models "obedient." ChatGPT's explosive popularity in November 2022 was partly due to GPT-3.5's capabilities, but RLHF's role in making the model feel more like a "willing assistant" rather than a "cold text completer" was an underrated key factor.

But RLHF also had a fundamental flaw: it depended on massive human annotation. OpenAI hired large numbers of annotators (many in low-wage countries like Kenya) to read harmful content generated by models and label "which answer is better." These workers received low pay and suffered psychological harm, yet their labor formed the foundation of ChatGPT's safety.[^3]

Moreover, as models grew more powerful, human judgment became increasingly insufficient. When a 540-billion-parameter model writes technical reasoning that humans struggle to understand, how can ordinary annotators judge whether it's right or wrong? RLHF paved the earliest road, but that road came to an end just slightly ahead of model capabilities.

## II. Constitutional AI: Replacing Humans with Rules

The limits of RLHF spurred an alternative: instead of having humans annotate piece by piece, what if we gave the model a "constitution" — a set of rules for it to judge right and wrong on its own?

Anthropic proposed Constitutional AI (CAI) in December 2022. The approach involved two steps. First, a set of behavioral principles (the Constitution) was used to have the model critique and revise its own responses — for example, "please determine whether this response contains discriminatory content, and if so, rewrite it." This generated a batch of high-quality self-corrected data. Second, this data was used to train a preference model, followed by final fine-tuning through RL.[^4]

The Constitution's content drew from multiple sources: the UN Declaration of Human Rights, Apple's terms of service, DeepMind's Sparrow rules, and Anthropic's own safety research. The philosophy behind this approach was: don't rely on the intuition of temporary annotators to judge "good" and "bad" — instead, rely on a set of pre-written, principled, auditable rules.

CAI accomplished at least three things historically. First, it significantly reduced dependence on human annotation, eliminating the painful process of "having people read harmful content and score it" in RLHF. Second, it made safety policies legible — the Constitution is a document that can be published, discussed, modified, and audited. Third, it inadvertently transformed AI safety from "engineers' internal adjustments" into a "governance question": who has the right to write the constitution? What goes in? What gets left out?

The Claude model series, starting with Claude 1 (March 2023), adopted Constitutional AI as its core safety framework. Anthropic's positioning has always been clear: we're not the "most capable model" — we're the "safest model." While ChatGPT and GPT-4 swept the market, Anthropic used "safety" as differentiation — a strategy that looked defensive in 2023 but became offensive after regulatory pressure intensified in 2024.

## III. The Governance Race: Governments Step In

After 2023, AI safety shifted from a voluntary exercise by tech companies to a mandatory requirement under government regulation.

**On 2023-10-30**, the White House released the "Executive Order on Safe, Secure, and Trustworthy Artificial Intelligence." This was the most comprehensive AI executive order from the U.S. federal government to date, covering safety testing standards, privacy protection, fairness, workforce impacts, and national security. The executive order required companies developing the most powerful AI systems to report safety testing results to the government.[^5]

**On 2023-11-01 to 11-02**, the first AI Safety Summit was held at Bletchley Park in the UK. Twenty-eight countries (including the United States, China, and the European Union) signed the "Bletchley Declaration," agreeing to cooperate internationally on AI safety. The historical significance of this declaration lay in its achievement against the backdrop of intensifying U.S.-China tech competition — the two countries agreed to cooperate at least on AI safety.[^6]

**On 2024-05-21 to 05-22**, the AI Seoul Summit was convened. Sixteen major AI companies (including OpenAI, Google, Microsoft, Amazon, Meta, and Anthropic) signed frontier AI safety commitments, including pausing development or deployment when model risks reach certain thresholds.[^7]

**On 2024-08-01**, the EU AI Act officially entered into force. This was the world's first comprehensive AI law, adopting a risk-tiered system: AI applications posing unacceptable risk are banned (such as social credit scoring), high-risk AI systems must meet strict compliance requirements, limited-risk systems require transparency, and minimal-risk systems face no additional obligations. The Act established a regulatory framework for general-purpose AI models and foundation models.[^8]

This sequence of actions demonstrated one thing: **AI safety moved from engineers' notebooks into congressional voting halls.** But the governance race also created a new tension: different countries regulate at different speeds. The EU moved fastest, passing a strict and specific set of laws; the United States advanced through executive orders rather than legislation, more flexible but more easily reversed by the next president; China managed large language model deployment through a registration system rather than a legal framework. Global AI governance is not a completed puzzle but three different sets of rules running in parallel on the same track.

## IV. SB 1047: The Rise and Fall of the California Bill

In 2024, the most dramatic episode in U.S. AI governance unfolded in California.

**SB 1047** (Safe and Secure Innovation for Frontier Artificial Intelligence Models Act) was introduced by California State Senator Scott Wiener. The bill's core requirements were: developers training large models (with training costs exceeding $100 million or using more than 10²⁶ FLOPS of compute) must conduct safety assessments before training, implement safety measures, retain auditable records, and bear liability for significant harms caused by their models.[^9]

This bill triggered a full-scale rift in the AI industry during the summer of 2024.

The supporters were primarily AI safety researchers and some academics: Geoffrey Hinton, Yoshua Bengio, and others publicly endorsed the bill, arguing that preventive regulation was the responsible approach — waiting for accidents before legislating would be too late. The opposition included virtually all of mainstream Silicon Valley: OpenAI, Anthropic, Google, and Meta all expressed opposition or reservations. The objections centered on several points: regulation should be federal rather than state-level, the bill focused excessively on hypothetical risks while neglecting current harms, and it could stifle open-source innovation. Anthropic's opposition to SB 1047 was particularly striking — a company whose brand was built on "safety" opposing a bill named after "safety."

On 2024-09-29, California Governor Gavin Newsom vetoed SB 1047. In his veto message, he stated that while the bill's intentions were commendable, its thresholds were too arbitrary — defining "high-risk" solely by training cost and compute scale, rather than assessing risk based on models' actual application scenarios. Newsom also indicated he would work with legislators, the federal government, and academia to advance a more reasonable regulatory approach.[^10]

The historical significance of SB 1047 lies not in whether it passed, but in making one thing clear: **no company can any longer say "AI safety doesn't need regulation."** The core dispute was never "whether to regulate" but "who regulates, by what standards, and when to act." The bill was vetoed, but it advanced the debate's framework from "self-regulation vs. government regulation" to "what kind of government regulation is reasonable."

## V. The Current Landscape: Three Parallel Lines

By 2026, the landscape of AI safety and alignment runs along roughly three parallel tracks.

**The first is the technical track.** RLHF and Constitutional AI remain mainstream, but new methods continue to emerge. RLAIF (using AI feedback to replace human feedback), scalable oversight, interpretability research (Anthropic's mechanistic interpretability team), red-teaming, and process reward models all attempt to answer the same question: when models are more capable than humans, how do humans supervise them? This question has no answer yet.

**The second is the governance track.** The EU AI Act is in execution, U.S. states and the federal government are in a tug-of-war, China is adjusting its registration system, and the UK is advancing its AISI (AI Safety Institute) model. The fragmentation of international governance creates a practical problem: a model can be trained in the United States, deployed in the EU, with data sourced globally — but which set of rules must it follow?

**The third is the contestation track.** "Safety" itself is becoming a battleground. OpenAI uses safety justifications to hide o1's chain-of-thought, Anthropic uses safety as a brand moat, open-source DeepSeek responds with public chain-of-thought arguing "transparency is safety," and each party's definition of "safety" is entirely different. Some people's safety means "preventing human extinction," others mean "protecting user privacy," and still others mean "compliance with laws and regulations." The consensus on "AI safety" extends only to "safety is needed" — but on the three critical questions of "what safety means, who defines it, and how to verify it," disagreements are widening.

## VI. The Safety Summit Series: The International Governance Race

The Bletchley Summit was only the beginning. Over the following two years, the international community launched a governance race around AI safety.

**On 2025-02-10 to 02-11**, France hosted the AI Action Summit in Paris. Unlike the consensus atmosphere at Bletchley, the Paris Summit exposed rifts: 61 countries signed a "Statement on Inclusive and Sustainable Artificial Intelligence for People and the Planet," but the United States and the United Kingdom refused to sign.[^11] U.S. Vice President J.D. Vance stated clearly at the summit that the United States would not accept an international framework that "over-regulates" AI. The UK said the statement "did not provide sufficient practical commitments on global governance and national security."

The failure of the Paris Summit exposed a structural contradiction in international AI governance: Bletchley achieved consensus because in November 2023, countries had not yet fully grasped the economic and geopolitical consequences of AI regulation. By February 2025, the EU AI Act was already in effect, and the United States faced the reality of AI regulation affecting chip exports and cloud services competition — "safety" was no longer a purely technical topic but directly related to control over supply chains. French President Macron attempted to push an international framework similar to the EU AI Act at the summit, but the U.S.-UK refusal to sign demonstrated: **safety governance involving supply chain control cannot achieve consensus through diplomatic summits.**

The Paris Summit marked the transition of international AI governance from a "consensus period" to a "fragmentation period" — the Bletchley photograph of 28 signatory nations became, two years later, a family portrait with absentees.

**In 2025-05**, South Korea hosted a follow-up meeting to the AI Safety Summit; the Netherlands had also hosted an AI Safety Summit in 2024. Countries began establishing their own AI Safety Institutes (AISIs) — the United States established an AISI under NIST, the UK was first to create an independent AISI, and Japan and Singapore joined as well. On the surface, this was an expansion of multilateral cooperation; in reality, each AISI had different assessment standards and governance philosophies. China's role in the summit series is worth noting. China attended the Bletchley Summit and signed the declaration — a rare gesture of U.S.-China cooperation in the AI safety domain. But China did not sign the Paris Summit statement. China prefers to participate in AI governance discussions through the United Nations framework (rather than Western-led summits). In 2024, the United Nations published "Governing AI for Humanity," and China actively participated in this process. The U.S.-China divergence on AI safety governance is not about "whether to regulate" but "who leads" — the United States favors industry self-regulation plus executive orders, the EU favors legal frameworks, and China favors a state-led registration system. Behind these three models lie three different state-market relationships.

The historical significance of the summit series lies in: **AI safety becoming a formal agenda item at international summits for the first time** — but being on the agenda does not equal consensus. Bletchley proved dialogue was possible; Paris proved consensus was fragile. Future international AI governance will likely not move toward a unified global framework but toward regionalization — an EU standards zone, a U.S. standards zone, a China standards zone — each with its own safety definitions and compliance requirements. This is not the ideal outcome, but it may be the realistic one.

## VII. The Biden-Trump Flip: The Politicization of Safety Definitions

On 2023-10-30, Biden signed the AI executive order (described in §III). On 2025-01-20, Trump was inaugurated. On 2025-01-21, Trump revoked Biden's AI executive order.[^12]

The revocation was not a replacement — Trump initially issued no alternative. For the industry, this meant that mandatory federal AI safety reporting requirements vanished overnight. Companies that had built compliance processes under the executive order (such as OpenAI, Google, and Anthropic, which had already submitted safety testing reports to the government) found that the rules they had been following suddenly no longer existed. The Seoul Summit commitments signed in 2024 — in which 16 AI companies pledged to pause development when model risks reached thresholds — lost their legal anchor at the federal level. The commitments remained on paper, but without the executive order's accompanying enforcement mechanism, they were more of a voluntary code of conduct than a regulatory framework.

**In 2025-07**, Trump signed a new AI executive order — but in an entirely different direction. This executive order centered on "preventing political bias in AI," requiring AI systems purchased by the federal government to be "politically neutral" and criticizing the previous administration's AI regulation as "suppressing innovation under the guise of safety." The executive order used the term "woke AI" multiple times, reframing AI safety as an ideological issue.[^13]

The implications of this framework switch are profound. The Biden executive order asked "Is this model safe?" — the question pointed at the model's technical behavior. The Trump executive order asks "Is this model neutral?" — the question pointed at the model's ideological stance. Both questions seek to identify AI's "defects," but "unsafe" and "not neutral" point to entirely different governance strategies. The former requires technical assessment, red-teaming, and incident reporting; the latter requires ideological review, political vetting, and content auditing. The former hands regulatory authority to technical experts; the latter hands it to political officials. This is not merely a change in regulatory direction — it is a replacement of the regulatory paradigm.

The historiographic significance of this flip lies not in which side is "right," but in exposing a structural problem: **when AI governance is built on executive orders rather than legislation, safety standards can flip overnight with a change in president.** Biden said "safety is a technical risk issue"; Trump said "safety is an ideological issue" — two uses of "safety" pointing in completely different regulatory directions.

The practical impact of the executive order flip on the industry is multidimensional. Companies that had built compliance systems under Biden's executive order found their investments partially sunk — OpenAI, Google, and Anthropic had all submitted safety testing reports to the government, with formats and content based on Biden executive order requirements; now the format standards themselves were uncertain. The more far-reaching impact was uncertainty in expectations: if the next president flips back again, how should companies plan compliance investments? This uncertainty is itself a form of regulatory cost — not from the strictness of rules but from their instability.

The fragility of U.S. AI governance lies not in a lack of rules but in the instability of rules themselves. By contrast, the EU AI Act is a legislative product that does not expire with changes in the European Commission; China's registration system is based on administrative inertia and does not interrupt with personnel changes. The UK's AISI model, while light-touch, at least has institutional endorsement. The executive order governance model makes the United States the most unpredictable variable in global AI regulation — for AI companies that need long-term compliance planning, this may be the worst governance model.

## VIII. The EU AI Act Takes Effect: The Costs and Benefits of Legislation First

On 2024-08-01, the EU AI Act officially entered into force (its framework described in §III). By 2025–2026, the Act moved from paper to enforcement.

The EU AI Act's risk-tiered system is its most innovative design. Risk is categorized into four tiers from highest to lowest:

- **Unacceptable risk**: Social credit scoring, mass biometric surveillance, AI systems that manipulate human behavior — directly banned;
- **High risk**: AI systems used in healthcare, judiciary, education, recruitment, and credit — must meet strict data governance, transparency, and human oversight requirements;
- **Limited risk**: Chatbots, deepfakes — must inform users they are interacting with AI;
- **Minimal risk**: Most AI applications — no additional obligations.

Additionally, the Act established a dedicated chapter for "general-purpose AI models" (GPAI), requiring all foundation model providers to disclose training data summaries, conduct model evaluations, and report serious incidents. For high-capability models with "systemic risk" (trained with compute exceeding 10²⁵ FLOPS), red-teaming, adversarial evaluation, and incident reporting are required.

Starting February 2025, the Act's prohibition provisions began enforcement. Starting August 2025, transparency obligations for general-purpose AI models began enforcement. Starting August 2026, all compliance requirements for high-risk AI systems will be fully enforced.[^14]

The EU AI Act's greatest challenge is not the text itself but enforcement capacity. EU member states vary enormously in regulatory resources — France and Germany can assemble professional AI review teams, while smaller Eastern European countries may struggle to recruit reviewers at all. Moreover, AI technology iterates far faster than laws can be revised — when an Act takes three years to complete the entire process from legislation to enforcement, model capabilities may have advanced across two or three generations. The Act's 10²⁵ FLOPS threshold (used to define models with "systemic risk") may already be easily surpassed by most frontier models by the time the Act is fully enforced — at which point the "high-capability model" threshold is no longer a threshold.

The Act's "Brussels Effect" — influencing global companies through market access requirements — is more effective than its internal enforcement: if OpenAI and Google want to operate in the EU market, they must comply with the EU AI Act regardless of whether the United States has similar laws. Meta's suspension of AI feature launches in the EU in 2024 due to data privacy issues is a direct manifestation of the Brussels Effect. OpenAI was temporarily banned in Italy (later lifted) for violating GDPR — the EU AI Act may replicate or even amplify this market access leverage.

The three paths of global AI regulation form a striking contrast:

- **United States**: Executive orders as the primary instrument, flexible but unstable. Presidential turnover can flip safety standards overnight;
- **European Union**: Legislation first, stable but complex to enforce. The most complete legal framework, but enforcement depends on member states' regulatory willingness and capability;
- **China**: Registration system, opaque but efficient. Companies submit model registrations to go live, with the government retaining post-hoc accountability. Governance rules are adjusted in practice rather than debated before legislation.

The UK took a fourth path — assessing risk through a technical institution (AISI) rather than enacting laws. This is a "light-touch" governance approach: AISI can publish risk assessment reports and recommend that companies take measures, but has no legal enforcement power. The U.S.-UK joint refusal to sign at the Paris Summit in February 2025 was partly because they believed the Paris statement leaned too heavily toward the EU-style legal framework.

The coexistence of these four paths creates a practical problem: **regulatory arbitrage.** AI companies can choose to train models in the most permissive jurisdiction and make only minimal compliance adjustments when deploying in stricter jurisdictions. For example, a model trained in Singapore (with virtually no AI-specific regulation) can meet the EU AI Act's transparency requirements through technical adjustments without meeting deeper safety assessments. This follows the same logic as multinational tax planning — the jurisdictional nature of laws allows globally deployed products to exploit regulatory differences and reduce compliance costs.

The EU AI Act's most far-reaching impact may lie not within the EU but in its "Brussels Effect" — when one of the world's largest AI markets (450 million EU consumers) demands certain safety standards, global companies will unify around those standards for market access. This is how GDPR became the global privacy standard. If the EU AI Act replicates this effect, it may become the de facto global AI safety standard — regardless of whether the United States and China acknowledge it.

## IX. The Founding of SSI: Safety as a Business Narrative

**On 2024-06-19**, Ilya Sutskever, together with Daniel Gross and Daniel Levy, founded Safe Superintelligence Inc. (SSI). The company's sole objective: building safe superintelligence.[^15]

The founding of SSI was itself a statement. Sutskever was a co-founder and former Chief Scientist of OpenAI who participated in the November 2023 board action that led to Sam Altman's removal — an action whose core demands included AI safety (see "The OpenAI Chronicle"). Altman returned within five days, and Sutskever officially left OpenAI in May 2024. After leaving, he chose the most extreme path: not to be "an AI company that also does safety research" but to be "an AI company that only does safety." SSI's product roadmap includes no chatbot, no API, no enterprise services — only safe superintelligence. While nearly all AI companies waver between "capability" and "safety," SSI chose to stand at only one end of the scale.

The capital market's response to this positioning was surprising. SSI's valuation reportedly reached $5 billion in 2025, making it one of the largest early-stage fundings in AI startups.[^16] A company with no products, no revenue, and "safety" as its sole selling point received a $5 billion valuation — this fact itself tells two things: first, Sutskever's personal brand is strong enough to support this valuation — as a co-author of the Transformer paper and OpenAI's former Chief Scientist, his technical credibility is virtually unquestioned; second, the capital market at least partially endorses the logic that "safety itself can be a business" — in the competitive convergence of AI capabilities, "safest" may become the next differentiation point.

But SSI's valuation also hints at a bubble risk: a company with no products receiving a $5 billion valuation is based on the consensus that "safety matters" — and this consensus itself may be fragile. If AI development advances slower than expected, the demand for "superintelligence safety" becomes less urgent, and SSI's valuation loses its anchor. If AI safety is solved by the open-source community or government institutions, SSI's commercialization path is cut off. SSI's valuation is the biggest bet in AI safety — not a bet on technology, but on timelines.

SSI's business narrative contains an internal contradiction: **if safety is the sole objective, what counts as "safe"?** Sutskever's definition of safety may differ from investors' definition, and from the government's definition. SSI has not yet released any models or technical reports, meaning its "safety" remains an empty vessel — capital has poured in a $5 billion valuation, but the vessel has not yet been filled with any substantive technical commitments.

The deeper question is: how can a company that "only does safety" sustain its valuation without producing products? The answer may be: SSI is betting on a time gap — occupying the "safety" niche before superintelligence arrives, so that when other companies produce near-AGI models, safety solutions become a scarce resource. This is a business model that treats safety as a futures trade. Whether it works depends on whether Sutskever's judgment about "when superintelligence arrives" is accurate — if arrival is far beyond expectations, SSI may exhaust capital patience before its product is born.

## X. The Superalignment Dilemma: OpenAI's Promise and Its Bankruptcy

If SSI represents the extreme of "making safety into a company," then the story of OpenAI's Superalignment team represents the structural failure of "doing safety within a company."

**In 2023-07**, OpenAI announced the establishment of the Superalignment team, co-led by Ilya Sutskever and Jan Leike. The team's goal was to solve the alignment problem for future superhuman AI systems. OpenAI committed to allocating 20% of its secured compute to this team.[^17]

Twenty percent of compute — this figure was widely reported at the time and became a symbol of OpenAI's "safety commitment." If truly honored, it would mean one-fifth of OpenAI's computing resources would be dedicated to safety research — in the context of a global compute shortage, this was a rather generous promise.

But the value of this commitment was quickly questioned. According to multiple reports, the compute actually allocated to the Superalignment team was far below 20%.[^18] The definition of "20% of compute" was itself ambiguous: 20% of all GPUs? 20% of the training budget? Or "can apply to use up to 20% when safety research requires it"? Different definitions correspond to vastly different actual resource amounts. In practice, the safety team had to compete with the product team for compute — and the product team had clear deadlines and revenue targets; the safety team did not.

**In 2024-05**, Ilya Sutskever and Jan Leike both left OpenAI in succession. After departing, Leike publicly stated on social media that OpenAI's "safety culture and processes have taken a backseat to shiny products."[^19] Sutskever subsequently founded SSI (see §IX). The Superalignment team was disbanded after the two departed, with members folded into other research groups.

In a safety testing report submitted to the U.S. government in July 2025, OpenAI stated that its models scored better than competing models in key safety evaluations.[^20] But the Superalignment team's story illustrates a deeper problem: **when safety work and product development compete for the same pool of compute and talent, safety almost always loses.** Not because the company "doesn't care about safety" — OpenAI's public statements have never been missing the word "safety" — but because in the actual decisions of resource allocation, safety's priority ranks after products. The 20% compute promise was an empty check not because OpenAI deliberately lied, but because under the pressure of product competition, that 20% could be "temporarily requisitioned" at any time.

In September 2024, OpenAI employees and former employees published an open letter calling on AI companies to provide stronger whistleblower protections and safety commitments for employees. Although the letter did not name OpenAI, its context was the dissolution of the Superalignment team and Leike's public statements. Reportedly, about half of OpenAI's employees signed the letter. When half a company's employees believe safety commitments need institutionalized protection, the company's own safety governance is difficult for outsiders to trust.

The bankruptcy of the "20% compute" promise reveals a deeper structural problem: **within a profit-driven company, safety is always a cost center, never a profit center.** The Superalignment team's work — researching how to align AI smarter than humans — does not directly generate revenue. When the product team needs compute to train GPT-5 or improve ChatGPT, safety research compute becomes a resource that can be "temporarily requisitioned." This is not unique to OpenAI — any commercially oriented AI company faces the same incentive structure. Anthropic's and Google DeepMind's safety teams face similar pressures, just to different degrees.

This brings us back to a fundamental question: can AI safety be left to the market?

SSI's $5 billion valuation says "yes" — safety can be a business. The dissolution of OpenAI's Superalignment team says "no" — within a company, safety always yields to products. Both are real signals, pointing to the same conclusion: **safety cannot rely solely on corporate self-discipline and also needs external governance — but external governance itself (as shown in §VI–§VIII) faces the dilemma of politicization and fragmentation.** Market, company, and government — all three paths are imperfect. This may not be a problem that can be "solved" but a risk that requires continuous management — and the capacity for risk management depends on whether we honestly face the reality that the word "safety" is being instrumentalized.

## XI. Restructuring the Current Landscape: The Triple Alienation of Safety

The landscape of 2023 could be summarized as "three parallel lines" (see original §V). By 2026, this landscape has been profoundly restructured — not that the three lines have multiplied, but that they have begun to intertwine and alienate one another. Safety has transformed from a technical objective into a narrative battlefield contested by multiple parties.

By 2026, the AI safety and alignment landscape is no longer three parallel lines as in 2023, but three lines intertwined and alienated at a higher dimension.

**The technical track** is advancing but has not broken through. RLHF remains mainstream — by 2026, virtually all frontier models use some form of human feedback training. Constitutional AI has reduced dependence on human annotation through rule-based self-correction. RLAIF (using AI feedback to replace human feedback) has further pushed automation — but RLAIF introduces a new problem: if the AI providing feedback is itself biased, bias is amplified rather than corrected. Anthropic's mechanistic interpretability team attempts to understand what's happening inside models — in 2024 they published a paper on identifying internal features within models, which is the most cutting-edge interpretability work to date. But even together, these technical tools cannot answer the core question: when models are more capable than humans, how do humans supervise them? This question remains as unanswered in 2026 as it was in 2023. Process Reward Models attempt to improve supervision by rewarding correct reasoning steps rather than only final answers — but they still depend on humans labeling "what counts as a correct step," and when model reasoning exceeds human comprehension, this dependency breaks.

**The governance track** is expanding but fragmenting. From Bletchley to Paris, AI safety summits moved from consensus to division. The U.S. executive order flip (§VII), the EU AI Act entering enforcement (§VIII), and China's registration system continuing to operate — these three governance paths are mutually incompatible. The UK AISI model attempts to offer a fourth option, but its joint refusal to sign with the United States at the Paris Summit suggests the UK leans more toward America's "light regulation" than the EU's "heavy regulation."

Global AI governance is not a completed puzzle but three different sets of rules running in parallel on the same track. A model trained in the United States, using Chinese data, deployed in the EU — which set of rules must it follow? Regulatory arbitrage (described in §VIII) makes this question even more acute. The dilemma of AI Safety Institutes (AISIs) shows that even with institutions in place, governance independence remains an untested hypothesis.

**The political track** is a new dimension and the most destructive. "Safety" has transformed from a technical objective into a political tool. Two successive U.S. presidents have used "safety" to justify diametrically opposite policies — one saying "safety is a technical risk," the other saying "safety is ideology." Anthropic uses "safety" to justify military cooperation. SSI uses "safety" to justify a $5 billion valuation. Bletchley and Paris use "safety" to justify two different international governance paths. When "safety" can be used to simultaneously justify contradictory positions, it ceases to be consensus — it is a territory repeatedly contested.

The intertwining of these three lines has produced several trends worth recording:

**The arms-racing of safety research.** Countries have begun treating AI safety research as part of national security. The United States establishes safety assessment standards through NIST AISI, essentially defining "which models are safe" — this definition directly affects which models can be deployed in the U.S. market. China implicitly defines "safety" through its registration system — models that don't pass registration are "unsafe." Safety assessment standards are becoming a form of non-tariff barrier — just as food safety standards can be used to block agricultural imports, AI safety standards can be used to block foreign AI models. U.S. chip export controls have already restricted China's compute; if safety assessment standards are also weaponized as a competitive tool, AI safety is completely transformed from a "shared human problem" into a "great power bargaining chip."

**The divorce of safety and capability.** Before 2023, "doing safety research" and "doing capability research" were usually the same people — safety was an inherent constraint on capability development. After 2024, the two paths began diverging: SSI does only safety, OpenAI's safety teams are shrinking, and Anthropic uses safety as a brand but allocates most resources to capability improvement. Safety research is transforming from "a team within a company" into "an independent niche" — this is both specialization (safety researchers can focus exclusively on it) and marginalization (safety is no longer embedded in the core process of capability development).

The most dangerous consequence of this divorce is the "differential progress problem": if capability research advances continuously faster than safety research, the gap between the two widens over time. Each capability breakthrough — GPT-4, o1, next-generation models — creates new safety challenges, while the development cycle for safety solutions is typically longer than for capability breakthroughs. This is neither technological optimism nor pessimism — it is a fact about research resource allocation. When a company allocates 90% of its compute to capability improvement and 10% to safety research, the differential is structural.

**The awkwardness of AI Safety Institutes.** Institutions like the UK AISI and the U.S. AISI face a fundamental dilemma: they are government agencies evaluating commercial companies' products. When evaluation results are unfavorable to a major company, does the government have sufficient political will to publish negative reports? AISI's independence — free from commercial interests and political pressure — is an untested hypothesis. The U.S.-UK refusal to sign at the 2025 Paris Summit suggests: when safety evaluation results conflict with national interests, the latter often prevails.

**The open-source safety paradox.** DeepSeek open-sourced R1 under the MIT license, and its public reasoning chain-of-thought was hailed as a model of "transparency as safety." But the safety challenges of open-source models differ from those of closed-source models — closed-source model safety strategies are centrally controlled by the vendor (updating refusal rules, system prompts, etc.), while open-source models, once distributed, have safety strategies no longer under the publisher's control. Anyone can remove safety guardrails and fine-tune away refusal behaviors. This is not an argument against open source — but it shows that the relationship between "open source" and "safety" is more complex than the "transparency is safety" narrative.

## Afterword

The history of AI safety is not an ascending straight line but a series of tug-of-war contests — between technical solutions and capability needs, between corporate self-discipline and government regulation, between international consensus and national interests.

RLHF taught models to listen to humans, but the limits of human annotation arrived quickly. Constitutional AI replaced humans with rules, but the power to make rules became a governance question. Governments stepped in, but differences in regulatory pace and standards caused fragmentation. SB 1047 was vetoed, but the framework it left behind — "large models need to be regulated" — has become consensus. Biden's executive order established a federal framework; Trump dismantled it two years later — proving the fragility of executive order governance. The EU AI Act took the legislative route, stable but complex to enforce, and the Brussels Effect may be its most far-reaching legacy.

The summit series from Bletchley to Paris proved one thing: countries agree to discuss AI risks but not how to govern them — the U.S.-UK refusal to sign at the Paris Summit moved AI safety governance from the consensus period into the fragmentation period. The Biden executive order to Trump flip proved another: when governance is built on executive orders rather than legislation, safety standards can flip overnight with a change in president — Biden's technical risk framework was replaced by Trump's ideological framework, not just a change in direction but a change in paradigm. The EU AI Act provided a third proof: legislation first is stable but complex to enforce, the Brussels Effect is more effective than internal enforcement, but legal texts cannot keep pace with model iteration. The non-coordination of these three paths is itself a risk — a model trained in the United States, using Chinese data, deployed in the EU — which set of rules must it follow?

SSI's $5 billion valuation proves safety can be a business narrative. The dissolution of OpenAI's Superalignment team proves "20% of compute for safety" is an empty check — not because the company deliberately lied, but because under product competition, safety's priority always ranks last. Anthropic's DoD controversy proves "safety first" gets reframed under geopolitical pressure — when your client is the Pentagon, who exactly is "safety" protecting? OpenAI's Superalignment team dissolution proves safety commitments can be empty checks in the face of product competition — no matter how beautiful the 20% compute figure sounds, without institutional guarantees it's just words on paper. DeepSeek's open-source reasoning chain proves "transparency is safety" can be an effective narrative strategy — but open-source model safety guardrails are not under the publisher's control, and the relationship between transparency and safety is far more complex than the narrative suggests.

What deserves the most vigilance is not the inadequacy of safety technology itself but the triple alienation of safety narratives.

**The first alienation: branding.** Anthropic uses "safety" to build a moat. When it simultaneously cooperates with the DoD, who exactly is "safety" protecting? The answer depends on how you define "safety" — is it "not harming users" or "not posing a threat to U.S. national security"? The two definitions aren't contradictory, but their emphasis is completely different. The danger of branding is: when safety becomes a brand, safety can no longer be allowed to fail — brands cannot fail, but science can.

**The second alienation: politicization.** Trump's "woke AI" executive order transformed safety from a technical risk issue into an ideological one. This is not a rhetorical change — it directly alters the direction of regulation. Biden's executive order focused on "will the model cause catastrophic risk"; Trump's executive order focuses on "does the model have political bias." Both questions have value, but they are not the same question. When "safety" is politicized, technical safety researchers face a dilemma: your research is used to say completely different things.

**The third alienation: fragmentation.** The summit series from Bletchley to Paris proves: countries can agree that "AI needs safety" but not what "safety" means. The EU's safety is legal compliance; America's safety is market freedom; China's safety is social stability. When every participant uses "safety" to refer to different things, international coordination becomes everyone talking past each other.

When "safety" can be used to simultaneously justify contradictory positions — OpenAI uses it to hide chain-of-thought, DeepSeek uses public chain-of-thought to argue "transparency is true safety"; Biden uses it to argue technical risk, Trump uses it to argue ideology; SSI uses it to justify a $5 billion valuation, the dissolution of OpenAI's Superalignment team argues "20% of compute for safety" is an empty check — what historians should do is not take sides but unpack each party's "safety": what is being restricted, who is being protected, and who gets to decide.

The future of AI safety will likely not converge on unity. Technical solutions will continue iterating — RLHF to RLAIF to pure RL, interpretability from surface features to deep circuits, process reward models attempting to reward reasoning steps beyond just answers. But even together, these technical tools cannot answer the unresolved question: when models are more capable than humans, how do humans supervise them? This is not a problem that can be solved once and for all — it is a question that must be continuously re-answered as capabilities grow. International governance will trend toward regionalization — EU standards zone, U.S. standards zone, China standards zone, each with its own safety definition. Safety narratives will continue to be contested — every party will say "we are the ones who truly care about safety." In this contest, the only thing worth trusting is not any party's declarations but what choices each party makes when facing real interest conflicts. SSI chose "only do safety" — the cost is having no products. OpenAI chose "both safety and products" — the cost was the safety team's dissolution. Anthropic chose "safety as a brand" — the cost is that the brand may be diluted by military cooperation. DeepSeek chose "safety as transparency" — public chain-of-thought, using openness in place of safety commitments. Every path has its cost; there is no perfect choice. But every choice is recorded in this chronicle.

The question this chronicle asks has never been "Is AI safety important?" — the answer is obviously yes. The question is: when every participant claims to "care most about safety," whose definition of safety will ultimately become the de facto standard? The answer depends not on whose definition is most "correct" but on who has the largest market, the strongest military, and the deepest capital. The EU AI Act's Brussels Effect may make the EU standard the global de facto standard, not because the EU's definition is the best but because the EU market is the largest. Trump's "woke AI" executive order may change the ideological orientation of global AI training, not because its definition is most reasonable but because the United States is the center of the AI industry.

Safety has never been a purely technical issue — it is a power issue. And the answer to power questions has never been in papers — it is in politics. In capital. In the strategic calculations of countries that did not sign the declarations at summits. In the quarterly earnings reports of companies that signed but did not honor their compute commitments. What historians can do is peel back every layer of "safety" to show readers the real structure beneath — and then let readers judge for themselves.
