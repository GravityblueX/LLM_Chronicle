# AI Safety and Alignment: From Technical Solutions to Governance Struggles

> Every capability leap in large language models simultaneously produces two things: excitement and fear. Excitement comes from "what it can do"; fear comes from "will it do something it shouldn't." AI safety and alignment represent humanity's ongoing effort to manage this fear — from the earliest RLHF teaching models to "listen to humans," to Constitutional AI replacing human feedback with rules, to governments worldwide attempting to draw hard boundaries for models through law. This is not a straight line — it is a series of tug-of-war.

## I. RLHF: Infusing human preferences into models

The safety alignment problem of large language models was first raised in a straightforward way: the model is powerful, but it does not necessarily obey you.

GPT-3 could write fluent essays, but also generate racially discriminatory, gender-biased, and violent content. It did not do so intentionally — it merely encountered such material in its training data and faithfully reproduced it. Pre-training taught models "how to speak" but did not teach them "what not to say."

**InstructGPT** was published by OpenAI in January 2022 under the title "Training language models to follow instructions with human feedback." It proposed the three-step pipeline that would become the industry standard: first collect human preference annotation data (human annotators rank multiple responses to the same prompt), then train a reward model on these preferences, and finally use reinforcement learning (the PPO algorithm) to adjust the language model so its generated responses better align with human preferences.[^1]

This pipeline was abbreviated as RLHF (Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback). It did more than "filter profanity." It taught the model something more subtle: what constitutes a "good answer." Human annotators did not merely tell the model "this sentence cannot be said" — they taught it "this answer is more helpful, more honest, and more harmless than that one."

Almost simultaneously, Anthropic published "Training a Helpful and Harmless Assistant with Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback." The paper explored in detail the tension between Helpful and Harmless — sometimes the information a user wants may itself be harmful, and the model must find a balance between the two.[^2]

RLHF occupies a special place in large model history. It was not the technology that made models "smart" but the technology that made models "obedient." ChatGPT's explosive popularity in November 2022 was due not only to GPT-3.5's capabilities but also to RLHF making the model more like a "willing assistant" rather than a "cold text completer" — an underappreciated key factor.

But RLHF also had a hard flaw: it depended on massive human annotation. OpenAI hired large numbers of annotators (many in low-wage countries like Kenya) to read harmful content generated by models and label "which answer is better." These workers earned low wages and suffered psychological harm, yet their labor was the cornerstone of ChatGPT's safety.[^3]

Moreover, as models grew stronger, human judgment became increasingly insufficient. When a 540B-parameter model writes technical reasoning that humans struggle to comprehend, how should an ordinary annotator judge whether it is right or wrong? RLHF laid the earliest groundwork, but the road ended just a short distance ahead of model capabilities.

## II. Constitutional AI: Replacing humans with rules

RLHF's limitations drove an alternative approach: instead of having humans annotate item by item, could we give the model a "constitution" and let it judge right from wrong on its own?

Anthropic proposed Constitutional AI (CAI) in December 2022. The approach had two steps. First, a set of behavioral principles (the Constitution) was used to have the model critique and revise its own responses — for example, "Please judge whether this response contains discriminatory content; if so, please rewrite it." This generated a batch of high-quality self-corrected data. Second, this data was used to train a preference model, followed by final fine-tuning through RL.[^4]

The Constitution's content drew from multiple sources: the UN Declaration of Human Rights, Apple's Terms of Service, DeepMind's Sparrow rules, and Anthropic's own safety research. The philosophy was: do not rely on the intuition of temporary annotators to judge "good" and "bad," but on a pre-written, principled, auditable set of rules.

CAI accomplished at least three things historically. First, it significantly reduced dependence on human annotation, eliminating the "have humans read harmful content and score it" layer of suffering in RLHF. Second, it made safety policies readable — the Constitution is a document that can be published, discussed, modified, and audited. Third, it inadvertently transformed AI safety from "internal engineering tuning" into a "governance question": who has the right to write the constitution? What goes in? What is omitted?

The Claude model series, starting with Claude 1 (March 2023), adopted Constitutional AI as its core safety framework. Anthropic's positioning has always been clear: we are not the "strongest model" — we are the "safest model." When ChatGPT and GPT-4 swept the market, Anthropic used "safety" as its differentiator — a strategy that looked defensive in 2023 but became offensive after regulatory pressure intensified in 2024.

## III. The governance race: Governments step in

After 2023, AI safety shifted from technology companies' voluntary exercise to a mandatory item for government regulation.

**October 30, 2023**, the White House issued the "Executive Order on Safe, Secure, and Trustworthy Artificial Intelligence." This was the most comprehensive AI executive order from the U.S. federal government to date, covering safety testing standards, privacy protection, fairness, workforce impact, and national security. The order required companies developing the most powerful AI systems to report safety testing results to the government.[^5]

**November 1–2, 2023**, the first AI Safety Summit was held at Bletchley Park, UK, with 28 countries (including the United States, China, and the EU) signing the "Bletchley Declaration," agreeing to cooperate internationally on AI safety. The historical significance of this declaration lay in its achievement against the backdrop of intensifying U.S.-China tech competition — both countries agreed to cooperate at least on AI safety.[^6]

**May 21–22, 2024**, the AI Seoul Summit convened, with 16 major AI companies (including OpenAI, Google, Microsoft, Amazon, Meta, Anthropic, and others) signing frontier AI safety commitments, including pausing development or deployment when model risks reach a certain threshold.[^7]

**August 1, 2024**, the EU AI Act officially entered into force. This was the world's first comprehensive AI legislation, adopting a risk-tiered system: AI applications posing unacceptable risk are banned (e.g., social credit scoring), high-risk AI systems must meet strict compliance requirements, limited-risk systems require transparency, and minimal-risk systems bear no additional obligations. The legislation established a regulatory framework for general-purpose AI and foundation models.[^8]

This sequence of actions demonstrated one thing: **AI safety moved from engineers' notebooks into congressional voting halls.** But the governance race also created a new tension: different countries regulated at different paces. The EU moved fastest, passing a strict and specific law; the United States advanced through executive orders rather than legislation, making it more flexible but more easily overturned by the next president; China managed large model deployment through a filing system rather than a legal framework. Global AI governance was not a completed jigsaw puzzle but three different sets of rules running in parallel on the same track.

## IV. SB 1047: The rise and fall of California's bill

In 2024, the most dramatic episode in U.S. AI governance unfolded in California.

**SB 1047** (Safe and Secure Innovation for Frontier Artificial Intelligence Models Act) was introduced by California State Senator Scott Wiener. The bill's core requirement: developers training large models (training costs exceeding $100 million or using more than 10²⁶ FLOPS) must conduct safety assessments before training, implement safety measures, retain auditable records, and bear liability for significant harms caused by the model.[^9]

This bill triggered a full-scale split in the AI industry during the summer of 2024.

Supporters were primarily AI safety researchers and some academics: Geoffrey Hinton, Yoshua Bengio, and others publicly endorsed it, arguing that preventive regulation was responsible and that waiting for accidents before legislating would be too late. Opponents encompassed virtually all of Silicon Valley's mainstream: OpenAI, Anthropic, Google, and Meta all expressed opposition or reservations. Objections centered on several points: regulation should be at the federal level rather than state level, excessive focus on hypothetical risks while neglecting present harms, and potential stifling of open-source innovation. Anthropic's opposition to SB 1047 was particularly noteworthy — a company whose brand was "safety" opposing a bill named for "safety."

On September 29, 2024, California Governor Gavin Newsom vetoed SB 1047. In his veto message, he stated that while the bill's intent was commendable, its thresholds were too arbitrary — defining "high risk" solely by training cost and compute scale rather than by models' actual application scenarios. Newsom also stated he would work with legislators, the federal government, and academia to advance a more reasonable regulatory approach.[^10]

SB 1047's historical significance lies not in whether it passed but in making one thing clear: **no company can any longer say "AI safety does not require regulation."** The core of the dispute was not "whether to regulate" but "who regulates, by what standards, and when to act." The bill was vetoed, but it advanced the framing of the debate from "self-regulation vs. government regulation" to "what kind of government regulation is reasonable."

## V. Current landscape: Three parallel tracks

By 2026, the AI safety and alignment landscape comprised roughly three parallel tracks.

**The first is the technical track.** RLHF and Constitutional AI remain mainstream, but new methods continuously emerge. RLAIF (using AI feedback to replace human feedback), scalable oversight, interpretability research (Anthropic's mechanistic interpretability team), red-teaming, and process reward models are all attempting to answer the same question: when models are stronger than humans, how do humans supervise them? This question still has no answer.

**The second is the governance track.** The EU AI Act is in enforcement; U.S. states and the federal government are in a tug-of-war; China is adjusting its filing system; the UK is advancing the AISI (AI Safety Institute) model. International governance fragmentation creates a practical problem: a model can be trained in the U.S., deployed in the EU, with data from across the globe — but which set of rules must it follow?

**The third is the controversy track.** "Safety" itself is becoming a battlefield. OpenAI uses safety justifications to conceal o1's chain of thought; Anthropic uses safety as a brand moat; open-source DeepSeek uses public chain of thought to argue "transparency is safety"; each party's definition of "safety" is entirely different. Some define safety as "preventing human extinction," others as "protecting user privacy," and still others as "complying with laws and regulations." The consensus on "AI safety" extends only to "safety is needed" — but on the three critical questions of "what safety means, who defines it, and how to verify it," the divergence is widening.

## VI. The safety summit series: The international governance race

Bletchley was merely the starting point. Over the following two years, the international community launched a governance race around AI safety.

**February 10–11, 2025**, France hosted the AI Action Summit in Paris. Unlike Bletchley's consensus atmosphere, Paris exposed fissures: 61 countries signed the "Statement on Inclusive and Sustainable Artificial Intelligence for People and the Planet," but the United States and the United Kingdom refused to sign.[^11] Vice President J.D. Vance explicitly stated at the summit that the United States would not accept an international framework of "excessive regulation" of AI. The UK stated the declaration "did not provide sufficient practical commitments regarding global governance and national security."

Paris's failure exposed a structural contradiction in international AI governance: Bletchley achieved consensus because in November 2023 countries had not yet fully grasped the economic and geopolitical consequences of AI regulation. By February 2025, the EU AI Act was in force, and the U.S. faced the reality that AI regulation affected chip exports and cloud services competition — "safety" was no longer a purely technical topic but directly concerned control over supply chains. French President Macron attempted to advance an EU AI Act-like international framework, but the U.S.-UK refusal to sign indicated: **safety governance involving supply chain control cannot achieve consensus through diplomatic summits.**

Paris marked international AI governance's transition from a "consensus period" to a "fragmentation period" — the photograph of 28 countries signing at Bletchley became, two years later, a family portrait with absences.

**May 2025**, South Korea hosted a follow-up AI safety summit meeting; the Netherlands also hosted an AI safety summit in 2024. Countries began establishing their own AI Safety Institutes (AISIs) — the U.S. under NIST, the UK first as an independent body, with Japan and Singapore joining. On the surface this was an expansion of multilateral cooperation, but in practice each AISI's evaluation criteria and governance philosophies differed. China's role in the summit series deserves attention. China attended the Bletchley Summit and signed the declaration — a rare cooperative gesture in U.S.-China AI safety. But China did not sign the Paris summit's statement. China prefers participating in AI governance discussions through the UN framework rather than Western-led summits. In 2024, the UN published "Governing AI for Humanity," and China actively participated. The U.S.-China divergence on AI safety governance is not about "whether to regulate" but "who leads" — the U.S. favors industry self-regulation plus executive orders, the EU favors legal frameworks, and China favors a state-led filing system. Behind these three models lie three different state-market relationships.

The summit series' historical significance lies in: **AI safety becoming a formal topic at international summits for the first time** — but being on the agenda does not mean achieving consensus. Bletchley proved dialogue possible; Paris proved consensus fragile. Future international AI governance will most likely not converge on a unified global framework but move toward regionalization — EU standard zones, U.S. standard zones, Chinese standard zones — each with its own safety definitions and compliance requirements. This is not the ideal outcome, but it may be the realistic one.

## VII. The Biden-Trump reversal: The politicization of safety's definition

On October 30, 2023, Biden signed the AI executive order (§III above). On January 20, 2025, Trump was inaugurated. On January 21, 2025, Trump revoked Biden's AI executive order.[^12]

The revocation was not a replacement — Trump initially issued no substitute. For the industry, this meant federal mandatory reporting requirements for AI safety vanished overnight. Companies that had built compliance processes under the executive order (OpenAI, Google, and Anthropic had all submitted safety testing reports to the government) found the rules they had been following suddenly nonexistent. The Seoul Summit commitments signed in 2024 — 16 AI companies promising to pause development when model risks reach a threshold — lost their legal anchor at the federal level. The commitments remained on paper, but without the executive order's accompanying enforcement mechanisms, they were more voluntary code of conduct than regulatory framework.

**July 2025**, Trump signed a new AI executive order — but with a diametrically different direction. This order centered on "preventing political bias in AI," requiring AI systems procured by the federal government to be "politically neutral" and criticizing the previous administration's AI regulation as "suppressing innovation under the guise of safety." The order repeatedly used the term "woke AI," reframing AI safety as an ideological issue.[^13]

The impact of this framework switch was profound. Biden's executive order asked "is this model safe?" — the question pointed at the model's technical behavior. Trump's executive order asked "is this model neutral?" — the question pointed at the model's ideological stance. Both questions sought "defects" in AI, but "unsafe" and "not neutral" point toward entirely different governance strategies. The former requires technical assessment, red-teaming, and incident reporting; the latter requires ideological review, political vetting, and content auditing. The former vests regulatory authority in technical experts; the latter vests it in political officials. This is not merely a change in regulatory direction — it is a replacement of the regulatory paradigm.

The historical significance of this reversal lies not in which side is "right" but in exposing a structural problem: **when AI governance rests on executive orders rather than legislation, safety standards can flip overnight with a change in president.** Biden said "safety is a technical risk question"; Trump said "safety is an ideological question" — two "safeties" pointing toward entirely different regulatory directions.

The practical impact of the executive order reversal on the industry was multidimensional. Companies that had built compliance systems under Biden's order found their investments partially sunk — OpenAI, Google, and Anthropic had all submitted safety testing reports whose format and content were based on Biden's order; now the format standards themselves were uncertain. The more far-reaching impact was uncertainty in expectations: if the next president reverses course again, how should companies plan compliance investments? This uncertainty is itself a regulatory cost — it comes not from strict rules but from unstable rules.

The fragility of U.S. AI governance lies not in the absence of rules but in the instability of rules themselves. By contrast, the EU AI Act is legislative and will not expire with a change in the European Commission; China's filing system runs on administrative inertia and will not be interrupted by personnel changes. The UK's AISI model, while light-touch, at least has institutional backing. The executive order governance model makes the United States the most unpredictable variable in global AI regulation — for AI enterprises requiring long-term compliance planning, this may be the worst governance model.

## VIII. EU AI Act in force: The costs and benefits of legislation-first

On August 1, 2024, the EU AI Act officially entered into force (its framework described in §III). By 2025–2026, the legislation moved from paper to enforcement.

The EU AI Act adopted a risk-tiered system — its most innovative design. Risks are classified into four tiers from highest to lowest:

- **Unacceptable risk**: social credit scoring, mass biometric surveillance, AI systems manipulating human behavior — directly banned;
- **High risk**: AI systems used in healthcare, judiciary, education, recruitment, credit — must meet strict data governance, transparency, and human oversight requirements;
- **Limited risk**: chatbots, deepfakes — must inform users they are interacting with AI;
- **Minimal risk**: most AI applications — no additional obligations.

Additionally, the legislation established a dedicated chapter for "general-purpose AI models" (GPAI), requiring all foundation model providers to disclose training data summaries, conduct model evaluations, and report serious incidents. For high-capability models with "systemic risk" (training compute exceeding 10²⁵ FLOPS), requirements include red-teaming, adversarial evaluation, and incident reporting.

Starting February 2025, the Act's prohibition clauses entered enforcement. Starting August 2025, GPAI transparency obligations enter enforcement. Starting August 2026, all compliance requirements for high-risk AI systems will be fully enforced.[^14]

The EU AI Act's greatest challenge is not the text itself but enforcement capability. EU member states' regulatory resources vary enormously — France and Germany can assemble professional AI review teams, while smaller Eastern European nations may struggle to gather reviewers. Moreover, AI technology iterates far faster than laws can be revised — when legislation takes three years to complete the full cycle from enactment to enforcement, model capabilities may have crossed two or three generations. The Act's 10²⁵ FLOPS threshold (used to define "systemic risk" models) may already be easily surpassed by most frontier models by the time the Act is fully enforced — at which point the "high-capability model" threshold ceases to be a threshold.

The Act's "Brussels Effect" — influencing global companies through market access requirements — is more effective than its internal enforcement: if OpenAI and Google wish to operate in EU markets, they must comply with the EU AI Act regardless of whether the U.S. has similar domestic legislation. Meta's 2024 suspension of AI feature launches in the EU over data privacy concerns was a direct manifestation of the Brussels Effect. OpenAI was temporarily banned in Italy (later lifted) for GDPR violations — the EU AI Act may replicate or even amplify this market-access leverage.

Global AI regulation's three paths stand in stark contrast:

- **United States**: Executive orders, flexible but unstable. A presidential change can flip safety standards overnight;
- **EU**: Legislation-first, stable but complex to enforce. The most complete legal framework, but enforcement depends on member states' regulatory willingness and capability;
- **China**: Filing system, opaque but efficient. Companies submit model filings and can go live; the government retains ex post accountability. Governance rules are adjusted in practice rather than debated before legislation.

The UK pursued a fourth path — evaluating risk through a technical institution (AISI) rather than enacting law. This is a "light-touch" governance approach: AISI can issue risk assessment reports and recommend that companies take measures, but it has no legal enforcement power. The U.S.-UK joint refusal to sign at the February 2025 Paris summit was partly because they considered the Paris declaration overly tilted toward the EU-style legal framework.

The coexistence of four paths produces a practical problem: **regulatory arbitrage.** AI companies can choose to train models in the most permissive jurisdiction and deploy in stricter jurisdictions with minimal compliance. For example, a model trained in Singapore (with virtually no AI-specific regulation) could meet the EU AI Act's transparency requirements through technical adjustments without satisfying deeper safety assessments. This parallels the logic of multinational tax planning — the jurisdictional nature of law allows globally deployed products to exploit regulatory differences to reduce compliance costs.

The EU AI Act's most far-reaching impact may lie not within the EU but in its "Brussels Effect" — when one of the world's largest AI markets (the EU's 450 million consumers) demands certain safety standards, global companies will uniformly implement those standards for market access. This is how GDPR became the global privacy standard. If the EU AI Act replicates this effect, it may become the de facto global AI safety standard — regardless of whether the United States and China acknowledge it.

## IX. SSI's founding: Safety becomes a commercial narrative

**June 19, 2024**, Ilya Sutskever co-founded Safe Superintelligence Inc. (SSI) with Daniel Gross and Daniel Levy. The company's sole objective: build safe superintelligence.[^15]

SSI's founding was itself a statement. Sutskever was OpenAI's co-founder and former Chief Scientist; in November 2023, he participated in the board action that led to Sam Altman's dismissal — one of that action's core demands being AI safety (see *The OpenAI Chronicle*). Altman returned within five days, and Sutskever officially left OpenAI in May 2024. After departing, he chose the most extreme path: not making "an AI company that also does safety research" but making "a company that only does safety." SSI's product roadmap includes no chatbot, no API, no enterprise services — only safe superintelligence. While virtually all AI companies waver between "capability" and "safety," SSI chose to stand on only one side of the scale.

The capital market's response to this positioning was surprising. SSI's 2025 valuation reportedly reached $5 billion, making it one of the largest early-stage financings in the AI startup space.[^16] A company with no products, no revenue, and "safety" as its sole selling point received a $5 billion valuation — this fact itself demonstrates two things: first, Sutskever's personal brand is strong enough to support this valuation — as co-author of the Transformer paper and OpenAI's former Chief Scientist, his technical credibility is virtually unchallenged; second, the capital market at least partially endorses the logic that "safety itself can be business" — in a competition where AI capabilities are converging, "safest" may become the next differentiator.

But SSI's valuation also implies bubble risk: a company without products receiving a $5 billion valuation is predicated on the consensus that "safety matters" — and that consensus itself may be fragile. If AI development progresses slower than anticipated, "safe superintelligence" demand becomes less urgent, and SSI's valuation loses its anchor. If AI safety is solved by open-source communities or government institutions, SSI's commercialization path is cut off. SSI's valuation is the biggest bet in the AI safety field — not a bet on technology, but on timelines.

SSI's commercial narrative contains an internal contradiction: **if safety is the sole objective, what counts as "safe"?** Sutskever's definition of safety may differ from investors' definition and from governments' definition. SSI has not yet released any model or technical report, meaning its "safety" remains an empty vessel — capital has poured $5 billion of valuation into it, but the vessel contains no substantive technical commitments.

The deeper question: how can a company that "only does safety" sustain its valuation without producing products? The answer may be: SSI is betting on a time differential — occupying the "safety" niche before superintelligence arrives, so that when other companies build models approaching AGI, safety solutions become scarce resources. This is a business model that treats safety as futures trading. Whether it can succeed depends on whether Sutskever's judgment about "when superintelligence arrives" is accurate — if arrival is far later than expected, SSI may exhaust capital patience before its product is born.

## X. The Superalignment dilemma: OpenAI's promise and its bankruptcy

如果说SSI是"把安全做成公司"的极端，那么OpenAI超级对齐团队的故事就是"在公司内部做安全"的结构性失败。

如果说SSI is the extreme of "making safety into a company," then the story of OpenAI's Superalignment team is the structural failure of "doing safety inside a company."

**July 2023**, OpenAI announced the formation of its Superalignment team, co-led by Ilya Sutskever and Jan Leike. The team's goal was to solve the alignment problem for future superhuman AI systems. OpenAI committed to allocating 20% of its secured compute to this team.[^17]

20% of compute — this figure was widely reported at the time and became a symbol of OpenAI's "safety commitment." If truly fulfilled, it would mean one-fifth of OpenAI's computational resources would be dedicated to safety research — a remarkably generous commitment against the backdrop of global compute scarcity.

But this commitment's substance was quickly questioned. According to multiple reports, the compute actually allocated to the Superalignment team fell far below 20%.[^18] The definition of "20% of compute" was itself ambiguous: 20% of all GPUs? 20% of the training budget? Or "can apply to use up to 20% when safety research requires it"? Different definitions correspond to vastly different actual resource quantities. In practice, safety teams had to compete with product teams for compute — and product teams had clear deadlines and revenue targets, while safety teams did not.

**May 2024**, Ilya Sutskever and Jan Leike successively departed OpenAI. Leike publicly stated on social media after leaving that "safety culture and processes have taken a backseat to shiny products" at OpenAI.[^19] Sutskever subsequently founded SSI (see §IX). The Superalignment team was disbanded after both left, with members absorbed into other research groups.

In OpenAI's July 2025 safety testing report submitted to the U.S. government, the company stated that its models scored better than competing models on key safety evaluations.[^20] But the Superalignment team's story illustrates a deeper problem: **when safety work and product development compete for the same compute and talent, safety almost always loses.** Not because the company "doesn't value safety" — OpenAI's public statements have never lacked the word "safety" — but because in actual resource allocation decisions, safety's priority ranks after products. The 20% compute commitment was an empty promise not because OpenAI deliberately lied, but because under product competition pressure, that 20% could be "temporarily requisitioned" at any time.

In September 2024, OpenAI employees and former employees published a joint letter urging AI companies to provide stronger whistleblower protections and safety commitments for employees. Although the letter did not name OpenAI, its backdrop was the Superalignment team's dissolution and Leike's public statements. Reportedly, approximately half of OpenAI's employees signed the letter. When half a company's employees believe safety commitments need to be institutionalized, the company's own safety governance becomes difficult for outsiders to trust.

The bankruptcy of the "20% of compute" promise reveals a deeper structural problem: **within a profit-driven company, safety is always a cost center, not a profit center.** The Superalignment team's work — researching how to align AI smarter than humans — did not directly generate revenue. When product teams needed compute to train GPT-5 or improve ChatGPT, safety research's compute became a resource that could be "temporarily requisitioned." This is not unique to OpenAI — any commercially oriented AI company faces the same incentive structure. Anthropic's and Google DeepMind's safety teams face similar pressures, only to different degrees.

This brings us back to a fundamental question: can AI safety be left to the market?

SSI's $5 billion valuation says "yes" — safety can be business. The dissolution of OpenAI's Superalignment team says "no" — inside companies, safety always yields to products. Both are genuine signals pointing to the same conclusion: **safety cannot rely solely on corporate self-discipline; it also requires external governance — but external governance itself (as shown in §VI–§VIII) faces politicization and fragmentation.** The market, companies, and government — none of the three paths is perfect. This may not be a problem that can be "solved" but a risk that requires continuous management — and the capacity for management depends on whether we honestly face the reality that the word "safety" is being instrumentalized.

## XI. Current landscape restructuring: The triple alienation of safety

The landscape of 2023 could be summarized as "three parallel tracks" (see §V original). By 2026, this landscape has been profoundly restructured — not because the tracks multiplied, but because they began to intertwine and alienate one another. Safety transformed from a technical objective into a narrative battlefield contested by multiple parties.

By 2026, the AI safety and alignment landscape was no longer three parallel tracks but three tracks interweaving and alienating each other at a higher dimension.

**The technical track** is advancing but has not broken through. RLHF remains mainstream — by 2026, virtually all frontier models use some form of human feedback training. Constitutional AI reduces dependence on human annotation through rule-based self-correction. RLAIF (using AI feedback to replace human feedback) further drives automation — but RLAIF introduces a new problem: if the AI providing feedback has biases, those biases are amplified rather than corrected. Anthropic's mechanistic interpretability team attempts to understand what models are doing internally — they published papers in 2024 on identifying internal features, the most frontier interpretability work to date. But these technical measures combined still cannot answer the core question: when models are stronger than humans, how do humans supervise them? This question remained as unanswered in 2026 as in 2023. Process Reward Models attempt to improve supervision by rewarding correct reasoning steps rather than only final answers — but they still depend on humans labeling "what counts as a correct step," and when model reasoning exceeds human understanding, this dependency breaks.

**The governance track** is expanding but fragmenting. From Bletchley to Paris, AI safety summits moved from consensus to fragmentation. The U.S. executive order reversal (§VII), the EU AI Act entering enforcement (§VIII), and China's filing system continuing to operate — three governance paths, mutually incompatible. The UK AISI model attempted to offer a fourth option but joint refusal to sign with the U.S. at Paris suggested the UK leaned toward America's "light regulation" rather than the EU's "heavy regulation."

Global AI governance is not a completed jigsaw puzzle but three different sets of rules running in parallel on the same track. A model trained in the U.S., with Chinese data, deployed in the EU — which set of rules must it follow? Regulatory arbitrage (described in §VIII) makes this question even sharper. The AISI dilemma illustrates that even with institutions, governance independence remains an unverified assumption.

**The political track** is the new dimension and the most destructive. "Safety" has been transformed from a technical objective into a political instrument. Two successive U.S. presidents used "safety" to justify diametrically opposed policies — one said "safety is technical risk," the other said "safety is ideology." Anthropic used "safety" to justify military cooperation. SSI used "safety" to justify a $5 billion valuation. Bletchley and Paris used "safety" to justify two different international governance paths. When "safety" can be used to simultaneously justify contradictory positions, it ceases to be consensus — it is a territory repeatedly contested.

The intertwining of the three tracks produces several trends worth recording:

**Safety research's arms-racing.** Countries are beginning to treat AI safety research as part of national security. The U.S. establishes safety evaluation standards through NIST AISI, effectively defining "which models are safe" — a definition that directly affects which models can be deployed in the U.S. market. China implicitly defines "safety" through its filing system — models that don't pass filing are "unsafe." Safety evaluation standards are becoming a non-tariff barrier — just as food safety standards can be used to block agricultural imports, AI safety standards can be used to block foreign AI models. U.S. chip export controls have already constrained China's compute; if safety evaluation standards are also weaponized as competitive tools, AI safety is completely transformed from a "shared human problem" into a "great power bargaining chip."

**The split between safety and capability.** Before 2023, "doing safety research" and "doing capability research" were typically the same people — safety was an inherent constraint on capability development. After 2024, these paths began diverging: SSI does only safety, OpenAI's safety team is shrinking, Anthropic uses safety as a brand but allocates most resources to capability improvement. Safety research is transforming from "a team within a company" into "an independent sub-field" — both specialization (safety researchers can focus exclusively on it) and marginalization (safety is no longer embedded in the core process of capability development).

The most dangerous consequence of this split is the "differential progress" problem: if capability research consistently advances faster than safety research, the gap between them widens over time. Each capability breakthrough — GPT-4, o1, the next generation — creates new safety challenges, while safety solutions typically take longer to develop than capability breakthroughs. This is not technological optimism or pessimism — it is a fact about research resource allocation. When a company allocates 90% of compute to capability improvement and 10% to safety research, the differential is structural.

**The awkwardness of AI Safety Institutes.** Institutions like the UK AISI and U.S. AISI face a fundamental dilemma: they are government bodies evaluating commercial companies' products. When evaluation results are unfavorable to a major company, does the government have sufficient political will to publish negative reports? AISI's independence — free from commercial interests and political pressure — is an assumption that has not yet been tested. The U.S.-UK refusal to sign at the 2025 Paris summit suggests: when safety evaluation results conflict with national interests, the latter takes priority.

**The open-source safety paradox.** DeepSeek open-sourced R1 under the MIT license, and its public reasoning chain of thought was celebrated as a model of "transparency as safety." But the safety challenges of open-source models differ from those of closed-source models — a closed-source model's safety policies are centrally controlled by the vendor (updating refusal rules, system prompts, etc.), while open-source models, once distributed, have safety policies no longer under the publisher's control. Anyone can remove safety guardrails or fine-tune away refusal behaviors. This is not an argument against open source — but it illustrates that the relationship between "open source" and "safety" is more complex than the "transparency is safety" narrative suggests.

## Commentary

The history of AI safety is not a rising straight line but a series of tug-of-war — between technical solutions and capability needs, between corporate self-discipline and government regulation, between international consensus and national interests.

RLHF taught models to listen to humans, but human annotation's limits arrived quickly. Constitutional AI replaced humans with rules, but the right to make rules became a governance question. Governments intervened, but differences in regulatory pace and standards created fragmentation. SB 1047 was vetoed, but the framework it left behind — "large models need regulation" — became consensus. Biden's executive order established a federal framework; Trump dismantled it two years later — proving the fragility of executive order governance. The EU AI Act took the legislative route, stable but complex to enforce; the Brussels Effect may be its most far-reaching legacy.

The summit series from Bletchley to Paris proved one thing: nations agree to discuss AI risk but not how to govern it — the U.S.-UK refusal to sign at Paris moved AI safety governance from the consensus period into the fragmentation period. Biden's executive order to Trump's reversal proved another: when governance rests on executive orders rather than legislation, safety standards can flip overnight with a change in president — Biden's technical risk framework replaced by Trump's ideological framework, not just a change in direction but a change in paradigm. The EU AI Act provided a third proof: legislation-first is stable but complex to enforce, the Brussels Effect is more effective than internal enforcement, but legal text cannot keep pace with model iteration. The non-coordination of three paths is itself a risk — a model trained in the U.S., with Chinese data, deployed in the EU: which set of rules must it follow?

SSI's $5 billion valuation proves safety can be a commercial narrative. The dissolution of OpenAI's Superalignment team proves "20% of compute for safety" is an empty check — not because the company deliberately lied, but because under product competition pressure, safety's priority always ranks last. Anthropic's DoD controversy proves "safety first" can be reframed under geopolitical pressure — when your customer is the Pentagon, who is "safety" protecting? DeepSeek's open-source reasoning chain proves "transparency is safety" can be an effective narrative strategy — but open-source model safety guardrails are not under the publisher's control, and the relationship between transparency and safety is far more complex than the narrative.

What warrants the greatest vigilance is not the insufficiency of safety technology itself but the triple alienation of safety's narrative.

**First alienation: Branding.** Anthropic uses "safety" to build a moat. When it simultaneously cooperates with the DoD, who is "safety" protecting? The answer depends on how you define "safety" — is it "not harming users" or "not threatening U.S. national security"? The two definitions are not contradictory but have entirely different focal points. The danger of branding is that when safety becomes a brand, safety can no longer fail — brands cannot fail, but science can.

**Second alienation: Politicization.** Trump's "woke AI" executive order transformed safety from a technical risk question into an ideological question. This is not a rhetorical change — it directly alters the direction of regulation. Biden's executive order focused on "will models cause catastrophic risk"; Trump's focuses on "do models have political bias." Both questions have value, but they are not the same question. When "safety" is politicized, technical safety researchers face a predicament: your research is used to say entirely different things.

**Third alienation: Fragmentation.** The summit series from Bletchley to Paris proved: nations can agree that "AI needs safety" but not what "safety" means. The EU's safety is legal compliance; America's safety is market freedom; China's safety is social stability. When every participant uses "safety" to denote different things, international coordination becomes each party talking past the others.

When "safety" can be used to simultaneously justify contradictory positions — OpenAI using it to hide chain of thought, DeepSeek using public chain of thought to argue "transparency is true safety"; Biden using it to argue technical risk, Trump using it to argue ideology; SSI using it to argue $5 billion in valuation, the dissolution of OpenAI's Superalignment team to argue "20% of compute for safety" is an empty check — what the historian should do is not take sides but peel back each party's "safety" to expose its real structure: what it restricts, who it protects, who decides.

The future of AI safety will most likely not converge. Technical solutions will continue iterating — RLHF to RLAIF to pure RL, interpretability from surface features to deep circuits, process reward models attempting to reward reasoning steps beyond answers. But these technical measures combined still cannot answer that hanging question: when models are stronger than humans, how do humans supervise them? This is not a problem that can be solved once and for all — it is a question that must be continuously re-answered as capabilities grow. International governance will move toward regionalization — EU standard zones, U.S. standard zones, Chinese standard zones, each with its own safety definition. The safety narrative will continue to be contested — each party will say "we are the ones who truly care about safety." In this contest, the only trustworthy thing is not any party's declaration but what choices each party makes when facing real conflicts of interest. SSI chose "only do safety" — the cost was having no product. OpenAI chose "safety and products together" — the cost was the safety team's dissolution. Anthropic chose "safety as brand" — the cost was the brand potentially being diluted by military cooperation. DeepSeek chose "safety as transparency" — public chain of thought, using openness in place of safety commitments. Every path has a cost; there is no perfect choice. But every choice is recorded in this chronicle.

What this chronicle has always sought to ask is not "is AI safety important" — the answer is obviously yes. What it seeks to ask is: when every participant claims to care most about safety, whose definition of safety will ultimately become the de facto standard? The answer does not depend on whose definition is most "correct" but on who has the largest market, the strongest military, the deepest capital. The EU AI Act's Brussels Effect may make the EU standard the global de facto standard — not because the EU's definition is best but because the EU's market is largest. Trump's "woke AI" executive order may alter the ideological orientation of global AI training — not because its definition is most reasonable but because the United States is the center of the AI industry.

Safety was never a purely technical question — it is a question of power. And the answer to a power question is never in papers — it is in politics. In capital. In the strategic calculations of countries that did not sign at the summits. In the quarterly earnings reports of companies that signed but did not fulfill compute commitments. What the historian can do is peel back every layer of "safety's" skin to show the reader the real structure beneath — and then let the reader judge.
