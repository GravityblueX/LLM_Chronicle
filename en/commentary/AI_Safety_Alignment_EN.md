# AI Safety and Alignment: From Technical Solutions to Governance Games

> Every capability leap in large language models simultaneously produces two things: excitement and fear. Excitement comes from "what it can do"; fear comes from "whether it might do something it shouldn't." AI safety and alignment represent humanity's ongoing effort to manage this fear — from the earliest RLHF teaching models to "listen to humans," to Constitutional AI replacing human feedback with rules, to governments worldwide attempting to draw hard boundaries for models with law. This is not a straight line but a series of tug-of-war struggles.

## I. RLHF: Pouring human preferences into models

The safety alignment problem for large language models was first raised in a straightforward way: models are powerful, but they don't necessarily obey you.

GPT-3 could write fluent essays but could also generate racist content, gender bias, and violent material. It wasn't intentional — it had simply seen these things in training data and faithfully reproduced them. Pre-training taught models "how to speak" but didn't teach them "what not to say."

**InstructGPT** was released by OpenAI in January 2022, with the paper titled "Training language models to follow instructions with human feedback." It proposed the three-step process that would become the industry standard: first collect human preference annotation data (human annotators rank multiple answers to the same prompt), then use these preferences to train a reward model, and finally use reinforcement learning (the PPO algorithm) to adjust the language model so its generated answers better align with human preferences.[^1]

This process was abbreviated as RLHF (Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback). It did more than "filter profanity." It taught models something more subtle: what constitutes a "good answer." Human annotators didn't just tell the model "you can't say this" — they taught it "this answer is more helpful, more honest, and more harmless than that one."

Almost simultaneously, Anthropic published "Training a Helpful and Harmless Assistant with Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback." The paper explored in detail the tension between Helpful and Harmless — sometimes the information a user wants may itself be harmful, and the model needs to find balance between the two.[^2]

RLHF occupies a special position in the history of large language models. It is not the technology that made models "smart" but the technology that made models "obedient." ChatGPT's explosive popularity in November 2022 was due not only to GPT-3.5's inherent capabilities but also to RLHF making the model more like a "willing assistant" rather than a "cold text completer" — an underestimated key factor.

But RLHF also had a fundamental flaw: it depended on massive human annotation. OpenAI hired large numbers of annotators (many in low-wage countries like Kenya) to read harmful content generated by models and label "which answer is better." These people earned low wages and suffered psychological harm, yet their labor was the cornerstone of ChatGPT's safety.[^3]

Moreover, as models became stronger, human judgment became increasingly insufficient. When a 540B-parameter model writes technical reasoning that humans struggle to understand, how can ordinary annotators judge whether it is right or wrong? RLHF paved the first road, but the road ended just ahead of model capabilities.

## II. Constitutional AI: Replacing humans with rules

RLHF's limitations drove an alternative: instead of having humans annotate item by item, could we give the model a "constitution" and let it judge right from wrong?

Anthropic proposed Constitutional AI (CAI) in December 2022. The approach had two steps. First, use a set of behavioral principles (the Constitution) to have the model critique and revise its own answers — for example, "please determine whether this answer contains discriminatory content; if so, please rewrite it." This generated a batch of high-quality self-corrected data. Second, train a preference model on this data, then do final fine-tuning through RL.[^4]

The Constitution's content drew from multiple sources: the UN Declaration of Human Rights, Apple's Terms of Service, DeepMind's Sparrow rules, and Anthropic's own safety research. The philosophy was: don't rely on the intuitions of temporary annotators to judge "good" and "bad"; instead, rely on a pre-written, principled, auditable set of rules.

CAI accomplished at least three things historically. First, it significantly reduced dependence on human annotation, eliminating the painful layer of "having people read harmful content and score it" from RLHF. Second, it made safety policies readable — the Constitution is a text that can be published, discussed, modified, and audited. Third, it inadvertently transformed AI safety from "engineers' internal adjustments" into a "governance question": who has the right to write the constitution? What goes in? What is left out?

The Claude model series, from Claude 1 (March 2023), has used Constitutional AI as its core safety framework. Anthropic's positioning has always been clear: we are not "the strongest model" — we are "the safest model." While ChatGPT and GPT-4 swept the market, Anthropic used "safety" as differentiation — a strategy that looked defensive in 2023 but became offensive after regulatory pressure intensified in 2024.

## III. The governance race: Governments step in

After 2023, AI safety transformed from technology companies' optional exercise to a mandatory item for government regulation.

**October 30, 2023**: The U.S. White House issued the "Executive Order on Safe, Secure, and Trustworthy Artificial Intelligence." This was the most comprehensive AI executive order from the U.S. federal government to date, covering safety testing standards, privacy protection, fairness, workforce impact, and national security. The order required companies developing the most powerful AI systems to report safety test results to the government.[^5]

**November 1–2, 2023**: The first AI Safety Summit was held at Bletchley Park, UK, with 28 countries (including the United States, China, and the EU) signing the Bletchley Declaration, agreeing to cooperate internationally on AI safety. The historical significance of this declaration lies in its achievement amid escalating U.S.-China tech competition — both countries agreed to cooperate at least on AI safety.[^6]

**May 21–22, 2024**: The AI Seoul Summit convened, with 16 major AI companies (including OpenAI, Google, Microsoft, Amazon, Meta, Anthropic, etc.) signing frontier AI safety commitments, including pausing development or deployment when model risks reach certain thresholds.[^7]

**August 1, 2024**: The EU AI Act officially entered into force. This was the world's first comprehensive AI law, adopting a risk-tiered system: AI applications with unacceptable risk are banned (such as social credit scoring), high-risk AI systems must meet strict compliance requirements, limited-risk systems require transparency, and minimal-risk systems face no additional obligations. The law established a regulatory framework for general-purpose AI models and foundation models.[^8]

This sequence of actions demonstrated one thing: **AI safety moved from engineers' notebooks into legislative voting chambers.** But the governance race also created a new tension: different countries regulate at different paces. The EU ran ahead, passing a strict and specific set of laws; the United States advanced through executive orders rather than legislation, more flexible but more easily overturned by the next president; China manages large language model deployment through a filing system rather than a legal framework. Global AI governance is not a completed puzzle but three different rule sets running in parallel on the same racetrack.

## IV. SB 1047: The rise and fall of California's bill

In 2024, the most dramatic episode in U.S. AI governance occurred in California.

**SB 1047** (Safe and Secure Innovation for Frontier Artificial Intelligence Models Act) was introduced by California State Senator Scott Wiener. The bill's core requirement was: developers training large language models (with training costs exceeding $100 million or using more than 10²⁶ FLOPS) must conduct safety assessments before training, implement safety measures, retain auditable records, and bear liability for significant harms caused by models.[^9]

This bill triggered a full-scale split in the AI industry during the summer of 2024.

Supporters were mainly AI safety researchers and some academics: Geoffrey Hinton, Yoshua Bengio, and others publicly supported it, arguing that preventive regulation was responsible practice and that waiting for accidents before legislating would be too late. Opponents included virtually all of Silicon Valley's mainstream: OpenAI, Anthropic, Google, and Meta all expressed opposition or reservations. Objections centered on several points: regulation should be at the federal level, not state-level; the bill focused too much on hypothetical risks while ignoring current harms; it could stifle open-source innovation. Anthropic's opposition to SB 1047 was especially striking — a company whose brand was "safety" opposing a bill named for "safety."

On September 29, 2024, California Governor Gavin Newsom vetoed SB 1047. In his veto message, he stated that while the bill's intent was commendable, its thresholds were too arbitrary — defining "high-risk" solely by training cost and compute scale, rather than evaluating risk based on models' actual application scenarios. Newsom also stated he would work with legislators, the federal government, and academia to advance a more reasonable regulatory approach.[^10]

SB 1047's historical significance lies not in whether it passed but in making one thing clear: **no company can again say "AI safety doesn't need regulation."** The core dispute was not "whether to regulate" but "who regulates, by what standards, and when to act." The bill was vetoed, but it advanced the debate's framework from "self-regulation vs. government regulation" to "what kind of government regulation is reasonable."

## V. The current landscape: Three parallel tracks

By 2026, the landscape of AI safety and alignment is roughly three tracks running in parallel.

**The first is the technical track.** RLHF and Constitutional AI remain mainstream, but new methods keep emerging. RLAIF (using AI feedback to replace human feedback), scalable oversight, interpretability research (Anthropic's mechanistic interpretability team), red-teaming, and process reward models all attempt to answer the same question: when models are stronger than humans, how do humans supervise them? This question still has no answer.

**The second is the governance track.** The EU AI Act is in execution; U.S. states and the federal government are in a tug-of-war; China is adjusting its filing system; the UK is advancing the AISI (AI Safety Institute) model. International governance fragmentation creates a practical problem: a model can be trained in the United States, deployed in the EU, with data from around the globe — but which set of rules must it follow?

**The third is the dispute track.** "Safety" itself is becoming a battlefield. OpenAI uses safety reasoning to hide o1's chains of thought; Anthropic uses safety as a brand moat; open-source DeepSeek uses public chains of thought to argue "transparency is safety" — each party's definition of "safety" is entirely different. Some people's safety means "preventing human extinction," others mean "protecting user privacy," and others mean "complying with laws and regulations." The consensus on "AI safety" stops at "safety is needed," but on the three critical questions of "what safety is, who defines it, and how to verify it," disagreements are widening.

## VI. The summit series: The international governance race

The Bletchley Summit was only the starting point. Over the following two years, the international community launched a governance race around AI safety.

**February 10–11, 2025**: France hosted the AI Action Summit in Paris. Unlike Bletchley's consensus atmosphere, the Paris Summit exposed fractures: 61 countries signed a "Statement on Inclusive and Sustainable AI for People and the Planet," but the United States and the UK refused to sign.[^11] Vice President J.D. Vance stated explicitly that the U.S. would not accept an international framework that "over-regulates" AI. The UK stated the declaration "did not provide sufficient practical commitments on global governance and national security."

The Paris Summit's failure exposed a structural contradiction in international AI governance: Bletchley reached consensus because in November 2023, countries had not yet fully grasped the economic and geopolitical consequences of AI regulation. By February 2025, the EU AI Act had entered force, and the U.S. faced the reality that AI regulation impacts chip exports and cloud services competition — "safety" was no longer a purely technical topic but directly concerned supply chain control. French President Macron attempted to advance an EU AI Act-style international framework, but the U.S.-UK refusal to sign indicated: **safety governance involving supply chain control cannot be achieved through diplomatic summits.**

The Paris Summit marked international AI governance's transition from "consensus period" to "fragmentation period" — Bletchley's photo of 28 countries signing, two years later, became a family portrait with missing members.

**May 2025**: South Korea hosted a follow-up AI safety summit meeting; the Netherlands also hosted an AI safety summit in 2024. Countries began establishing their own AI Safety Institutes (AISIs) — the U.S. under NIST, the UK establishing an independent AISI first, Japan and Singapore joining. On the surface this was an expansion of multilateral cooperation; in practice, each AISI's evaluation criteria and governance philosophy differ. China's role in the summit series deserves attention. China attended the Bletchley Summit and signed the declaration — a rare cooperative gesture between the U.S. and China on AI safety. But China did not sign the Paris Summit statement. China prefers participating in AI governance discussions through the UN framework rather than Western-led summits. In 2024, the UN published "Governing AI for Humanity," and China actively participated. The U.S.-China divergence on AI safety governance is not about "whether to regulate" but "who leads" — the U.S. favors industry self-regulation plus executive orders, the EU favors legal frameworks, and China favors a state-led filing system. Behind these three models lie three different state-market relationships.

The summit series' historical significance lies in: **AI safety became an official topic of international summits for the first time** — but being on the agenda does not equal consensus. Bletchley proved dialogue is possible; Paris proved consensus is fragile. Future international AI governance will most likely not lead to a unified global framework but toward regionalization — EU standard zone, U.S. standard zone, Chinese standard zone — each with its own safety definitions and compliance requirements. This is not the ideal outcome, but it may be the realistic one.

## VII. The Biden-Trump flip: Politicization of safety definition

On October 30, 2023, Biden signed the AI executive order (described in §3). On January 20, 2025, Trump took office. On January 21, 2025, Trump rescinded Biden's AI executive order.[^12]

The rescission was not a replacement — Trump initially issued no alternative. For the industry, this meant federal mandatory reporting requirements for AI safety vanished overnight. Companies that had built compliance processes under the executive order (OpenAI, Google, and Anthropic had all submitted safety test reports to the government) found the rules they followed suddenly nonexistent. The Seoul Summit commitments signed in 2024 — 16 AI companies pledging to pause development when model risks reach thresholds — lost their legal anchor at the U.S. federal level. The commitments remained on paper, but without the executive order's accompanying enforcement mechanism, they became more voluntary code of conduct than regulatory framework.

**July 2025**: Trump signed a new AI executive order — but pointing in an entirely different direction. This order centered on "preventing political bias in AI," requiring AI systems purchased by the federal government to be "politically neutral," and criticizing the previous administration's AI regulation as "suppressing innovation in the name of safety." The order repeatedly used the term "woke AI," reframing AI safety as an ideological issue.[^13]

The implications of this framework switch are profound. Biden's executive order asked "Is this model safe?" — a question about the model's technical behavior. Trump's executive order asks "Is this model neutral?" — a question about the model's ideological stance. Both questions seek AI's "flaws," but "unsafe" and "not neutral" point to entirely different governance strategies. The former requires technical assessment, red-teaming, and incident reporting; the latter requires ideological review, political vetting, and content auditing. The former hands regulatory authority to technical experts; the latter to political officials. This is not merely a change in regulatory direction — it is a replacement of regulatory paradigm.

The historiographic significance of this flip lies not in which side is "right" but in exposing a structural problem: **when AI governance is built on executive orders rather than legislation, safety standards can flip overnight with a change of president.** Biden said "safety is a technical risk issue"; Trump said "safety is an ideological issue" — two "safeties" pointing in entirely different regulatory directions.

The executive order flip's practical impact on the industry is multidimensional. Companies that had built compliance systems under Biden's order found their investment partially sunk — OpenAI, Google, and Anthropic had all submitted safety test reports whose format and content were based on Biden's order's requirements; now the format standard itself was uncertain. The more profound impact is expectation uncertainty: if the next president flips again, how should companies plan compliance spending? This uncertainty is itself a regulatory cost — not from strict rules but from unstable rules.

The vulnerability of U.S. AI governance lies not in lacking rules but in the rules themselves being unstable. By contrast, the EU AI Act is a legislative product and will not expire with an EU Commission change; China's filing system is based on administrative inertia and will not be interrupted by personnel changes. The UK's AISI model is light-touch but at least has institutional backing. The executive order governance model makes the United States the most unpredictable variable in global AI regulation — for AI companies needing long-term compliance planning, this may be the worst governance model.

## VIII. EU AI Act implementation: Costs and benefits of law-first approach

On August 1, 2024, the EU AI Act officially entered into force (its framework described in §3). By 2025–2026, the law moved from paper to enforcement.

The EU AI Act uses a risk-tiered system, its most innovative design. Risk is divided into four tiers from highest to lowest:

- **Unacceptable risk**: Social credit scoring, mass biometric surveillance, AI systems manipulating human behavior — directly banned;
- **High risk**: AI systems used in healthcare, justice, education, recruitment, credit — must meet strict data governance, transparency, and human oversight requirements;
- **Limited risk**: Chatbots, deepfakes — must inform users they are interacting with AI;
- **Minimal risk**: Most AI applications — no additional obligations.

Additionally, the law establishes a dedicated chapter for "general-purpose AI models" (GPAI), requiring all foundation model providers to disclose training data summaries, conduct model evaluations, and report serious incidents. For high-capability models with "systemic risk" (training compute exceeding 10²⁵ FLOPS), red-teaming, adversarial evaluation, and incident reporting are required.

From February 2025, the law's prohibitive provisions began enforcement. From August 2025, GPAI transparency obligations began enforcement. From August 2026, all compliance requirements for high-risk AI systems will be fully enforced.[^14]

The EU AI Act's greatest challenge is not the provisions themselves but enforcement capacity. EU member states' regulatory resources vary enormously — France and Germany can assemble professional AI review teams, while smaller Eastern European countries may struggle to assemble reviewers at all. Moreover, AI technology iterates far faster than laws can be revised — when a law takes three years to complete the full process from legislation to enforcement, model capabilities may have advanced two or three generations. The law's 10²⁵ FLOPS threshold (used to define models with "systemic risk") may already be easily surpassed by most frontier models when the law is fully enforced — at which point the "high-capability model" threshold ceases to be a threshold.

The law's "Brussels Effect" — influencing global companies through market access requirements — is more effective than its internal enforcement: if OpenAI and Google want to operate in the EU market, they must comply with the EU AI Act regardless of whether the U.S. has similar laws. Meta's 2024 decision to pause launching AI features in the EU over data privacy issues is a direct manifestation of the Brussels Effect. OpenAI was temporarily banned in Italy (later lifted) for GDPR violations — the EU AI Act may replicate or even amplify this market access leverage.

Global AI regulation's three paths form a stark contrast:

- **United States**: Executive order-based, flexible but unstable. Presidential turnover can flip safety standards overnight;
- **EU**: Law-first, stable but complex to execute. The most complete legal framework, but enforcement depends on member states' regulatory willingness and capability;
- **China**: Filing system, opaque but efficient. Companies submit model filings to go live; the government retains post-hoc accountability. Governance rules are adjusted in practice rather than debated before legislation.

The UK took a fourth path — evaluating risk through a technical institution (AISI) rather than making laws. This is "light-touch" governance: AISI can publish risk assessment reports and recommend measures but has no legal enforcement power. The U.S.-UK joint refusal to sign at the February 2025 Paris Summit was partly because they considered the Paris declaration too biased toward the EU-style legal framework.

The coexistence of four paths produces a practical problem: **regulatory arbitrage.** AI companies can choose to train models in the least restrictive jurisdiction and make minimal compliance when deploying in stricter ones. For example, a model trained in Singapore (virtually no AI-specific regulation) can meet the EU AI Act's transparency requirements through technical adjustments without meeting deeper safety assessments. This is the same logic as multinational tax planning — the jurisdictional nature of law allows globalized products to exploit regulatory differences to reduce compliance costs.

The EU AI Act's most far-reaching impact may not be within the EU but through its "Brussels Effect" — when one of the world's largest AI markets (450 million EU consumers) demands certain safety standards, global companies will uniformly implement that standard for market access. This is how GDPR became the global privacy standard. If the EU AI Act replicates this effect, it may become the de facto global AI safety standard — regardless of whether the U.S. and China acknowledge it.

## IX. SSI's founding: Safety as commercial narrative

**June 19, 2024**: Ilya Sutskever co-founded Safe Superintelligence Inc. (SSI) with Daniel Gross and Daniel Levy. The company's sole goal: building safe superintelligence.[^15]

SSI's founding was itself a statement. Sutskever was OpenAI's co-founder and former chief scientist who participated in the November 2023 board action leading to Sam Altman's dismissal — a core demand of which was AI safety (see *The OpenAI Chronicle*). Altman returned within five days; Sutskever officially left OpenAI in May 2024. After leaving, he chose the most extreme path: not making "an AI company that also does safety research" but making "a company that only does safety." SSI's product roadmap has no chatbot, no API, no enterprise services — only safe superintelligence. While virtually all AI companies waver between "capability" and "safety," SSI chose to stand at one end of the scale only.

The capital market's response to this positioning was surprising. SSI's 2025 valuation reportedly reached $5 billion, making it one of the largest early-stage financings in AI.[^16] A company with no products, no revenue, and "safety" as its only selling point received a $5 billion valuation — this fact itself tells two things: first, Sutskever's personal brand is strong enough to support this valuation — as a Transformer paper co-author and OpenAI's former chief scientist, his technical credibility is nearly unassailable; second, the capital market at least partially endorses the logic that "safety itself can be a business" — in the competition where AI capabilities are converging, "safest" may become the next differentiation point.

But SSI's valuation also implies bubble risk: a company with no products receiving a $5 billion valuation is based on the consensus that "safety matters" — and this consensus itself may be fragile. If AI development is slower than expected, "superintelligence safety" becomes less urgent, and SSI's valuation loses its anchor. If AI safety is solved by open-source communities or government institutions, SSI's commercial path is cut off. SSI's valuation is AI safety's biggest bet — not on technology, but on timeline.

## X. The Superalignment dilemma: OpenAI's promise and bankruptcy

If SSI is the extreme of "making safety into a company," the story of OpenAI's Superalignment team is the structural failure of "doing safety inside a company."

**July 2023**: OpenAI announced the formation of the Superalignment team, co-led by Ilya Sutskever and Jan Leike. The team's goal was to solve alignment for future superhuman AI systems. OpenAI committed to allocating 20% of its secured compute to this team.[^17]

20% compute — this number was widely reported at the time and became a symbol of OpenAI's "safety commitment." If truly fulfilled, it meant one-fifth of OpenAI's compute resources would be dedicated to safety research — a generous commitment in the context of global compute scarcity.

But this commitment's substance was soon questioned. According to multiple reports, the actual compute allocated to the Superalignment team fell far below 20%.[^18] The definition of "20% compute" itself was ambiguous: 20% of all GPUs? 20% of the training budget? Or "can apply to use up to 20% when safety research needs it"? Different definitions correspond to vastly different actual resource amounts. In practice, safety teams had to compete with product teams for compute — and product teams had clear deadlines and revenue targets while safety teams did not.

**May 2024**: Ilya Sutskever and Jan Leike both left OpenAI. After leaving, Leike publicly stated on social media that OpenAI's "safety culture and processes have taken a backseat to shiny products."[^19] Sutskever subsequently founded SSI (see §9). The Superalignment team was disbanded after both left, with members absorbed into other research groups.

OpenAI stated in its July 2025 safety test report to the U.S. government that its models scored better than competing models in key safety evaluations.[^20] But the Superalignment team's story illustrates a deeper problem: **when safety work and product development compete for the same compute and talent, safety almost always loses.** Not because the company "doesn't care about safety" — OpenAI's public statements have never lacked the word "safety" — but because in actual resource allocation decisions, safety's priority ranks after products. The 20% compute promise was an empty check not because OpenAI intentionally lied but because under product competition pressure, this 20% could be "temporarily requisitioned" at any time.

In September 2024, OpenAI employees and former employees published an open letter calling on AI companies to provide stronger whistleblower protections and safety commitments. Though it did not name OpenAI, the context was the Superalignment team's dissolution and Leike's public statements. Reportedly, approximately half of OpenAI's employees signed. When half a company's employees believe safety commitments need institutional protection, the company's own safety governance is difficult for outsiders to trust.

The "20% compute" promise's bankruptcy reveals a deeper structural problem: **in a profit-driven company, safety is always a cost center, not a profit center.** The Superalignment team's work — researching how to align AI smarter than humans — does not directly generate revenue. When product teams need compute to train GPT-5 or improve ChatGPT, safety research compute becomes a resource that can be "temporarily requisitioned." This is not unique to OpenAI — any commercially oriented AI company faces the same incentive structure.

This brings us back to a fundamental question: can AI safety be left to the market?

SSI's $5 billion valuation says "yes" — safety can be a business. The dissolution of OpenAI's Superalignment team says "no" — inside a company, safety always defers to products. Both are genuine signals pointing to the same conclusion: **safety cannot rely solely on corporate self-discipline; it also needs external governance — but external governance itself (as shown in §6–§8) faces politicization and fragmentation challenges.** Market, corporate, and governmental paths are all imperfect. This may not be a problem that can be "solved" but a risk that requires continuous management — and the ability to manage risk depends on whether we honestly face the reality that "safety" is being weaponized.

## XI. Current landscape reconstruction: The triple alienation of safety

The 2023 landscape could be summarized as "three parallel tracks" (see §5 original). By 2026, this landscape has been profoundly reshaped — not that there are more tracks, but that the three tracks have begun to entangle and alienate each other. Safety has transformed from a technical objective into a narrative battlefield contested by multiple parties.

**The technical track** is advancing but without breakthroughs. RLHF remains mainstream — by 2026, virtually all frontier models use some form of human feedback training. Constitutional AI reduces dependence on human annotation through rule-based self-correction. RLAIF (replacing human feedback with AI feedback) further pushes automation — but RLAIF introduces a new problem: if the AI providing feedback has biases, those biases are amplified rather than corrected. Anthropic's mechanistic interpretability team attempts to understand what models are doing internally — they published papers in 2024 on identifying internal features, the most frontier interpretability work to date. But these technical measures together still cannot answer the core question: when models are stronger than humans, how do humans supervise them? Process Reward Models attempt to improve supervision by rewarding correct reasoning steps rather than just final answers — but they still depend on humans annotating "what counts as a correct step," and when model reasoning exceeds human comprehension, this dependency breaks.

**The governance track** is expanding but fragmenting. From Bletchley to Paris, AI safety summits moved from consensus to fracture. U.S. executive order reversal (§7), EU AI Act entering enforcement (§8), China's filing system continuing — three governance paths that are mutually incompatible. The UK AISI model attempts to offer a fourth option but jointly refused to sign with the U.S. at Paris, suggesting the UK leans toward U.S.-style "light regulation" rather than EU-style "heavy regulation."

**The political track** is a new dimension and the most destructive. "Safety" has been transformed from a technical objective into a political tool. Two U.S. presidents used "safety" to argue for opposite policies — one saying "safety is a technical risk," the other saying "safety is ideology." Anthropic used "safety" to justify military cooperation. SSI used "safety" to justify a $5 billion valuation. Bletchley and Paris used "safety" to justify two different international governance paths. When "safety" can simultaneously justify contradictory positions, it is no longer consensus — it is territory repeatedly contested.

**The arms race-ification of safety research.** Countries are beginning to treat AI safety research as part of national security. The U.S. establishes safety evaluation standards through NIST AISI, essentially defining "which models are safe" — a definition that directly impacts which models can be deployed in the U.S. market. China implicitly defines "safety" through its filing system — models that don't pass filing are "unsafe." Safety evaluation standards are becoming a non-tariff barrier.

**The safety-capability split.** Before 2023, "doing safety research" and "doing capability research" were usually the same people — safety was an inherent constraint on capability development. After 2024, these two paths diverged: SSI does only safety; OpenAI's safety team is shrinking; Anthropic uses safety for branding but devotes most resources to capability improvement. Safety research is transitioning from "a team inside a company" to "an independent subdomain" — both specialization (safety researchers can focus exclusively) and marginalization (safety is no longer embedded in core capability development processes).

The most dangerous consequence of this split is the "differential progress" problem: if capability research advances faster than safety research, the gap between them widens over time.

## Commentary

The history of AI safety is not an ascending line but a series of tug-of-war struggles — between technical solutions and capability needs, between corporate self-discipline and government regulation, between international consensus and national interests.

RLHF taught models to listen to humans, but human annotation's limits were quickly reached. Constitutional AI replaced humans with rules, but rule-making became a governance question. Governments intervened, but differences in regulatory pace and standards created fragmentation. SB 1047 was vetoed, but the framework it left — "large language models need regulation" — has become consensus. Biden's executive order established a federal framework; Trump dismantled it two years later — proving the fragility of executive order governance. The EU AI Act took the legislative route, stable but complex to execute; the Brussels Effect may be its most far-reaching legacy.

The Bletchley-to-Paris summit series proved one thing: countries agree to discuss AI risks but disagree on how to govern them. The Biden-to-Trump flip proved another: when governance is built on executive orders rather than legislation, safety standards can flip overnight with a change of president. The EU AI Act provided a third proof: law-first is stable but complex to execute. The incoordination of three paths is itself a risk.

SSI's $5 billion valuation proved safety can be a commercial narrative. The dissolution of OpenAI's Superalignment team proved "20% compute for safety" was an empty check — not because the company intentionally lied, but because under product competition pressure, safety's priority always ranks last. DeepSeek's open-source reasoning chains proved "transparency is safety" can be an effective narrative strategy — but open-source models' safety guardrails are not controlled by the publisher, and the relationship between transparency and safety is far more complex than narratives suggest.

The most alarming trend is not the inadequacy of safety technology itself but the triple alienation of safety narrative.

**First alienation: Branding.** Anthropic uses "safety" to build its moat. When it simultaneously cooperates with the DoD, whose safety is being protected? The answer depends on how you define "safety" — "not harming users" or "not threatening U.S. national security"? The two definitions are not contradictory but have entirely different emphases. The danger of branding is: when safety becomes a brand, safety can no longer fail — brands cannot fail, but science can.

**Second alienation: Politicization.** Trump's "woke AI" executive order transformed safety from a technical risk issue into an ideological issue. This is not a rhetorical change — it directly alters regulatory direction. Biden's order focused on "whether models pose catastrophic risks"; Trump's order focuses on "whether models have political biases." Both questions have value, but they are not the same question.

**Third alienation: Fragmentation.** The Bletchley-to-Paris summit series proved: countries can agree that "AI needs safety" but disagree on "what safety means." The EU's safety is legal compliance; the U.S.'s safety is market freedom; China's safety is social stability. When each participant uses "safety" to mean different things, international coordination becomes everyone talking past each other.

Safety has never been a purely technical problem — it is a power problem. And the answer to power problems has never been in papers. It is in politics. In capital. In the strategic calculations of countries that didn't sign at the summits. In the quarterly earnings reports of companies that signed but didn't fulfill their compute commitments. What the historian can do is peel back every layer of "safety" to show the reader the real structure underneath — then let the reader judge for themselves.
