# Treatise: Synthetic Data

> The training data of large models once came from natural internet corpora — web pages, books, code, forums. But as the boundaries of high-quality natural data gradually closed in, an alternative emerged: using data generated by models to train models. Is this a cure or a poison? The fact is, the answer depends on who is synthesizing, how they are synthesizing, and what they are synthesizing.

---

## I. Overview

Synthetic data, in the context of large model training, refers to training data generated by models or algorithms rather than directly sourced from natural human activity. It is not a new concept — GANs, data augmentation, and simulator-generated data all fall under the broad umbrella of synthetic data — but in the large model era, it has acquired a new meaning: **using the output of one large model as the input for another**.

The core tension here is: if a model learns from its own output, is it accumulating knowledge or accumulating bias? If the quality of synthetic data is high enough, can it break through the ceiling of natural data? If the quality is insufficient, will repeated training lead to "model collapse"?

This treatise records the evolution of synthetic data's role in large model training from late 2022 to 2025 — from peripheral experiment to mainstream practice, from instruction tuning extending to reasoning capabilities, from "cost-saving substitute" to "critical means for capability breakthroughs."

---

## II. Self-Instruct and the Synthetic Revolution in Instruction Alignment (2022–2023)

Synthetic data first entered the public eye at scale through the scenario of **instruction tuning**.

**2022-12-20**: Yizhong Wang et al. published the **Self-Instruct** paper on arXiv.[^1] The core idea was remarkably simple: use GPT-3 to generate triples of instruction, input, and output, then fine-tune the model with this synthetic data. The paper experimented on GPT-3, and after fine-tuning with 52K synthetic instruction examples generated by Self-Instruct, the model showed significant improvements on instruction-following benchmarks like SuperNI, **approaching the performance of human-annotated data**.

Self-Instruct's historical significance lay in proving one thing: **expensive human annotation is not needed — a model can generate alignment data good enough on its own**. This directly gave rise to the Alpaca phenomenon that followed.

**2023-03-13**: Stanford HAI released **Alpaca** — a model based on LLaMA 7B, fine-tuned with 52K instruction examples generated using the Self-Instruct method.[^2] Alpaca's synthetic data was generated by GPT-3.5 at a total cost of approximately $500.[^2] Its performance on multiple benchmarks approached GPT-3.5, while its training cost was less than one-thousandth of the latter.

Alpaca triggered two consequences:

1. **The LLaMA ecosystem explosion**: It proved that small models + high-quality synthetic data could approximate large model performance, catalyzing the open-source community's fine-tuning wave around LLaMA;
2. **A legal gray zone**: Using OpenAI's API outputs to train competing models directly violated OpenAI's terms of service prohibiting the use of outputs to train competing models.[^3] This was one of the earliest legal disputes over synthetic data.

## III. Microsoft's Phi Series: An Extreme Experiment in Data Quality (2023–2024)

If Self-Instruct proved that "synthetic data can replace human annotation," then Microsoft's **Phi series** advanced a more radical claim: **data quality may matter more than model scale**.

**2023-06-20**: Microsoft Research released **Phi-1**, with the paper titled "Textbooks Are All You Need."[^4] Phi-1 was a 1.3B-parameter code generation model whose training data consisted primarily of "textbook-quality" synthetic text generated by GPT-3.5 — not simple Q&A pairs, but structured, progressively complex programming tutorials. Phi-1 achieved a pass@1 of 50.6% on the HumanEval code benchmark, approaching GPT-4's level, while its model size was less than one percent of the latter.[^4]

Phi-1 stunned the industry. Its implication was: **if the data is good enough, small models can do what large models do**.

Microsoft continued iterating on this path:

| Date | Model | Parameters | Core Data Strategy | Key Metric |
|------|-------|------------|-------------------|------------|
| 2023-06 | Phi-1 | 1.3B | GPT-3.5 synthetic "textbook" data | HumanEval 50.6% pass@1 |
| 2023-09 | Phi-1.5 | 1.3B | Synthetic data extended to general language tasks | Multiple benchmarks matching 5–10x larger models |
| 2023-12 | Phi-2 | 2.7B | Synthetic data + curated web data | Multiple benchmarks matching 25x larger models |
| 2024-04 | Phi-3 | 3.8B | Synthetic data + web data filtering | Surpassed all same-scale models |
| 2024-12 | Phi-4 | 14B | Enhanced synthetic data pipeline + RLHF | Significant improvement on reasoning tasks |

A hidden thread running through the Phi series is the **continuous evolution of synthetic data production methods**: from directly having GPT-3.5 write tutorials, to designing complex "seed → filter → generate → validate" pipelines, to having small models generate drafts that are then scored and filtered by larger models. Synthetic data was no longer something achievable with a single prompt — it had become an engineering pipeline requiring meticulous design.

---

## IV. Model Collapse: The Dark Side of Synthetic Data (2023–2024)

As the industry surged toward synthetic data, counter-evidence was also accumulating.

**2023-05**: Ilia Shumailov et al. published "The Curse of Recursion: Training on Generated Data Makes Models Forget," demonstrating that when a model is repeatedly trained on its own generated data, the distribution gradually collapses — high-frequency patterns are continuously amplified while low-frequency (and typically most informative) patterns are forgotten.[^5] They termed this phenomenon **model collapse**.

The implications of this finding were severe: if one model publishes synthetic content on the internet and another model scrapes data from the internet to train, the synthetic data would "pollute" the natural data pool — the entire model ecosystem could gradually degrade, like repeatedly photocopying a photocopy.

**2024-07-24**: A follow-up paper published in *Nature* mathematically proved the theoretical inevitability of model collapse: even perfectly calibrated generative models will lose distributional tail information through repeated self-training iterations.[^6]

But the nuance here is critical: **model collapse occurs in uncontrolled, recursive synthetic data training, not in carefully designed and human-curated synthetic data pipelines**. The reason the Phi series did not exhibit collapse was that its synthetic data had clear pedagogical objectives, source diversity, and quality filtering mechanisms — it was not "let the model write and then learn from itself," but "let a strong model write textbooks for a weaker model."

Distinguishing between "controlled synthesis" and "recursive pollution" is the key to understanding the ethics and engineering of synthetic data.

## V. DeepSeek-R1: Reinforcement Learning for Generating Reasoning Chains (2025)

The most recent and perhaps most important application of synthetic data occurred in the domain of **reasoning capabilities**.

**2025-01-22**: DeepSeek released DeepSeek-R1.[^7] R1's training process included two key steps closely related to synthetic data:

1. **DeepSeek-R1-Zero**: Trained entirely through reinforcement learning (RL) to generate reasoning chains, **without using any human-annotated supervised fine-tuning data**. Through a loop of attempt → receive reward → improve in the RL environment, the model spontaneously learned to "display its reasoning process" (chain-of-thought).[^7] These reasoning chains were themselves synthetic data — they were not written by humans but created by the model during training.

2. **R1 distillation**: DeepSeek used R1 to generate approximately 800,000 high-quality reasoning examples, which were used to distill a series of small models (based on Qwen2.5 and Llama 3, ranging from 1.5B to 70B).[^7] These distilled models excelled on reasoning tasks, with training costs far below those of training reasoning capabilities from scratch.

DeepSeek-R1 advanced the application of synthetic data from "instruction following" to "reasoning capabilities" — a harder and more valuable domain. Its key insight was: **RL can serve as an engine for synthetic data**. Models do not need humans to provide examples of reasoning processes; they only need a reward signal to explore reasoning strategies on their own, then solidify those strategies into distillable synthetic data.

Combined with DeepSeek-V3's ultra-low training cost (2.788M H800 GPU hours), this formed the technical foundation of the "DeepSeek moment" in early 2025.[^8]

## VI. The Data Wall and the Next Step of Scaling (2024–2025)

Another driver of synthetic data was a more prosaic reality: **natural data may not be enough**.

Epoch AI's 2024 research estimated that the total volume of publicly accessible high-quality English text data was approximately 4–10 trillion tokens, while frontier models' training data had already reached 13–18T tokens (Qwen2.5 at 18T, Llama 3.1 at 15T).[^9] At current consumption rates, **high-quality natural data could face substantive exhaustion by 2026–2028**.

This does not mean text on the internet will disappear — it means models have already trained on most of the available high-quality text. To continue expanding training data, one must either use lower-quality web data (with diminishing returns) or use synthetic data.

NVIDIA's **Nemotron-4 340B** (2024-06) exemplified this trend: it used **98% synthetic data** for alignment training (generated by Nemotron-4 340B's own Instruct variant) and achieved performance approaching GPT-4.[^10]

Hugging Face's **Cosmopedia** (2024-02) offered another perspective: it used Mixtral 8x7B to generate over 25 billion tokens of synthetic textbook data covering science, history, mathematics, and other topics, released as an open pre-training dataset.[^11]

By mid-2025, the question about synthetic data was no longer "whether to use it" but "how to use it":

- **For pre-training**: Supplementing natural data shortfalls and expanding knowledge coverage;
- **For alignment**: Replacing human annotation to generate instruction-following data;
- **For reasoning**: Using RL to generate reasoning chains, then distilling them into smaller models;
- **For domain-specific applications**: Generating training data for data-scarce verticals (medical, legal, low-resource languages).

---

## VII. Trend Analysis

- **From substitute to mainstream**: In 2022, synthetic data was "a substitute when there isn't enough human annotation"; by 2025, it was a standard component of frontier model training. This shift happened in less than three years.
- **From simple to engineered**: Self-Instruct generated data with a single prompt; Phi-4 and Nemotron use complex multi-stage pipelines — generate → filter → validate → regenerate. Synthetic data production has become an engineering discipline.
- **Quality stratification**: Not all synthetic data is useful. The Phi series proved that "textbook-quality" synthetic data has extremely high value, but unfiltered model outputs used for training can cause degradation. Quality control has replaced data volume as the core competitive advantage.
- **RL is the new engine for synthetic data**: DeepSeek-R1 demonstrated that reinforcement learning can generate high-quality reasoning data without relying on human examples. This extended synthetic data's applicability from "imitating humans" to "surpassing human annotation" — because RL-discovered reasoning strategies may lie beyond the knowledge scope of human annotators.
- **Ethical boundaries are tightening**: The legal controversy over using competitor API outputs to train models emerged as early as 2023. As synthetic data becomes mainstream practice, the pressure for data provenance and licensing compliance will only increase.

---

## Commentary

The story of synthetic data reveals an easily overlooked fact of the large model era: a model's intelligence comes not only from the volume of data but from the design of data. The Phi series proved this with what 1.3B parameters achieved; DeepSeek-R1 proved this with RL-generated reasoning chains. Conversely, model collapse research reminds the industry that not everything "written by oneself" has learning value. The essence of synthetic data is an educational engineering project — strong models write textbooks for weaker models; good textbooks produce good students, while poor textbooks produce obsessive parrots. The reality of 2025 is that this production line has been proven: from instruction following to reasoning capabilities, from general domains to vertical scenarios, the penetration of synthetic data is irreversible. The remaining question is not "whether to use it" but "who has the ability to produce good textbooks" — data engineering has become the new compute.
